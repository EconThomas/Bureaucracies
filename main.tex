\begin{abstract}\noindent
We study how the distribution of beneficiary stakes affects the output a bureaucracy secures when monitoring is costly and shared. The model treats monitoring the agency as a public good among beneficiaries. When beneficiaries are more numerous or stakes are more equal, each captures less of the return from monitoring. Aggregate monitoring falls, bureaucratic effort falls, and output declines. We test this prediction using administrative data on agricultural leases for co-owned allotted trust land administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA). Lease income per trust acre measures the output the BIA secures for owners, who receive income in proportion to inherited ownership interests. Because unobserved land productivity, original allottee characteristics, and family histories may affect both ownership fractionation and lease income, we instrument fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. In the preferred instrumental-variables specification, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers lease income per trust acre by about 14 percent, compared with 2 to 3 percent in least-squares estimates. Additional descriptive patterns are consistent with the monitoring interpretation. The estimates show that dispersed claims reduce the output a shared bureaucratic agent secures, consistent with weaker beneficiary oversight.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On allotted Indian trust land, a single agricultural tract can have hundreds of owners. In the lease records we study, the median co-owned tract has 18 owners, 730 tracts have more than 100, and one has 1,248. The owners divide the rent that the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) secures for the tract in proportion to their inherited interests.

How does the distribution of beneficiaries' stakes affect a bureaucracy's output? Bureaucrats often have discretion over how much effort to devote to individual claims, services, and enforcement tasks. Political principals provide broad oversight, but the people most affected by an agency's decisions often monitor those decisions directly by attending hearings, contacting the agency, filing complaints, or writing to decision-makers \citep{Wilson1989}. Monitoring is costly. When one bureaucratic decision produces a shared output, each beneficiary captures only part of the gain from monitoring and has reason to leave that monitoring to others \citep{Olson1965}.

We formalize this logic as a public-goods problem in monitoring. Beneficiaries receive shares of a common bureaucratic output and may make costly monitoring contributions that raise bureaucratic effort. The model predicts that dispersed claims reduce monitoring and output: more beneficiaries or more equal stakes lower each beneficiary's private return to monitoring. With unequal stakes and common monitoring costs, the largest stake determines the equilibrium monitoring contribution, up to ties. The model also implies that lower monitoring costs raise monitoring and output because cheaper oversight leads the beneficiary with a given stake to monitor the agency more.

We test these predictions using administrative records on agricultural leases for co-owned allotted trust land administered by the BIA. Inheritance has divided many tracts into undivided ownership interests, meaning fractional claims to the whole tract rather than demarcated physical pieces. Owners receive lease income in proportion to these inherited interests, so they are the beneficiaries in the model. We call the dispersion of their ownership shares ownership fractionation. When an owner contacts the BIA, supplies information about land use, requests a higher rent, or monitors compliance, all owners benefit through the same tract-level lease income. Lease income per trust acre therefore measures the output the BIA secures for owners on the trust portion of the tract, while ownership shares determine owners' incentives to monitor the agency. Because the data cover signed leases, we study this intensive margin, the income a lease secures, not the extensive margin of whether a tract is leased at all.

Ownership structure is endogenous. Unobserved land productivity, original allottee characteristics, and family histories may affect both the ownership structure a tract inherits and the lease income it commands. Our main design instruments ownership fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. Probate orders divide a deceased owner's undivided interest among heirs, so their timing predicts ownership structure. Conditional on reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects, the design compares tracts in the same reservation rental market whose probate histories unfolded at different times. A causal interpretation requires that the residual timing variation be conditionally unrelated to unobserved determinants of lease income and affect income only through ownership fractionation. We probe threats to conditional independence and the no-direct-effect condition with lag-restricted instruments, finer local fixed effects, allotment-cohort controls, and area-weighted terrain and soil measures. We separately assess ownership measurement with a second snapshot, use weak-instrument-robust inference, and report leave-one-reservation-out checks. The preferred IV sample excludes the Crow Reservation because its competency-leasing regime can place some agricultural leases outside the BIA bargaining channel central to the model; we also report estimates that include Crow.

The main instrumental-variables estimate shows that ownership fractionation lowers lease income per trust acre. In the preferred specification, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers income by about 14 percent. The corresponding least-squares estimates imply a decline of about 2 to 3 percent. The larger IV estimate is consistent with omitted-variable bias pushing least squares toward zero: if lower-productivity land both commands lower rents and leaves poorer families with fewer surviving heirs, then less fractionated tracts look low-income for reasons unrelated to monitoring. Because the IV estimate is local to tracts whose ownership structure responds to probate timing, we do not interpret it as a global average effect for all allotted trust land. Alternative IV specifications using the same probate-timing instruments give the same qualitative pattern when the endogenous ownership measure is the largest owner's share, inverse largest-owner share, the square root and the log of the number of owners, the cumulative share of the largest five owners, or the ownership Herfindahl index. The estimated effect is largest among tracts with 2 to 30 owners, where doubling the owner count lowers lease income by about 11 percent.

Additional evidence is consistent with the monitoring interpretation, but it does not separately identify monitoring. The co-owners are dispersed principals who share the return from overseeing a common BIA agent. Least-squares estimates show higher lease income when ownership is more concentrated, while descriptive associations for tribal largest-owner status and direct payment are consistent with lower monitoring costs or greater owner involvement. Owners' holdings on other agricultural tracts are not associated with lower income, which points to free-riding within the tract rather than attention divided across tracts. The data do not record owner monitoring or BIA responses. Fractionation can also lower lease income through accumulated investment: tracts with many co-owners, each of whom would capture only a share of the return to improving the land, may have received less investment over time and so command lower rents even with the same monitoring. These results help interpret the mechanism but remain secondary to the probate-timing IV.

To scale the estimates, we use the model to compare fractionated ownership with a benchmark in which the same tract is monitored as if the ownership public-good problem were absent. Applying the preferred IV design to lease income measured in dollars gives an implied income gap of roughly 9 to 14 percent, or about \$260 to \$390 per lease-year, depending on whether the gap is evaluated at the mean largest-owner share or averaged across leases. Translating the preferred log estimate into dollars gives a larger range, roughly 15 to 24 percent, or about \$430 to \$670 per lease-year. Because these translations require choices about the dollar outcome and the evaluation point, we use the 40-to-20-share effect, about 14 percent, as the main causal magnitude. We also report a simpler model calibration based on a dollar regression without controls or fixed effects. That calibration implies a smaller gap of 3.0 percent of lease income, or about \$85 per lease-year, which we treat as a lower benchmark.

The paper contributes to the study of bureaucracy by shifting attention from political oversight to beneficiary monitoring of shared bureaucratic output. Existing work studies how Congress, the President, and agency objectives shape bureaucratic behavior \citep{Fiorina1977, KiewietMcCubbins1991, Weingast1984, Moe1985, WestCooper1989, Niskanen1971, Dixit2002, GailmardPatty2012}. Other work studies street-level discretion, intrinsic motivation, and biased or over-demanding clients \citep{Lipsky2010, BesleyGhatak2005, Prendergast2003, Prendergast2007, ForandUjhelyiTing2022}. In much of this work, client interactions are private: clients monitor to obtain services or benefits for themselves. We study a different multiprincipal agency problem. A bureaucracy produces one output for a group of beneficiaries, and monitoring by any one beneficiary raises the return to all. The public-good problem among dispersed principals can therefore shape the output of a common agent even within one administrative regime. Field experiments show that community monitoring can improve public services: in Uganda it increased health workers' effort and improved child health \citep{BjorkmanSvensson2009}, while in Indonesian village road projects government audits reduced missing expenditures but grassroots monitoring did so only where free-rider problems and elite capture were limited \citep{Olken2007}. We study that free-rider problem directly, using variation in how beneficiaries' stakes are distributed rather than an intervention that changes monitoring.

The paper also contributes to work on American Indian land tenure and fragmented ownership. \citet{LeonardParker2021} study oil production on the Fort Berthold Reservation and show that fragmented ownership reduces the use of a spatially expansive natural resource. The closest related paper is \citet{DippelFryeLeonard2025}, who estimate the long-run cost of nontransferable trust title by comparing allotted-trust parcels with nearby fee-simple parcels and measuring development and cultivation with satellite data. We study a different margin inside the trust regime: how observed ownership fractionation on leased allotted trust tracts affects the lease income the BIA secures. Their paper estimates the cost of restrictions on transfer rights. We estimate the bureaucratic-output cost of fragmented beneficiary stakes, holding trust status fixed. Reservation institutions also shape development: \citet{Dippel2014} shows that reservations that combined autonomous bands under shared governance are about 30 percent poorer today. Our comparisons are within reservations, so such reservation-level institutions are held fixed.

The estimates are most informative about leased, multi-owner agricultural tracts on allotted trust land where the BIA acts as the lease administrator, mostly in Montana, the Dakotas, and Oklahoma. They do not speak to wholly tribal land, unleased land, grazing permits, oil and gas leases, or reservations that were never allotted. Within the relevant population, the lease records are close to a census of the activity to which the mechanism applies. The broader lesson is that beneficiary-side collective action can affect how much output a bureaucracy secures even among claims administered by the same agency.

\section{Theory} \label{Theory}

Consider a bureaucracy that produces output $R$ for a group of $n$ beneficiaries. The bureaucracy is the agent. The beneficiaries are principals who receive shares of its output and may monitor its effort. A beneficiary may contribute monitoring, denoted by $m_i$, and we refer to a beneficiary who chooses $m_i>0$ as a monitoring contributor. Output increases with bureaucratic effort $e$, and effort increases with aggregate monitoring $M$.
\footnote{This assumption follows \citet{Prendergast2003} and \citet{Lipsky2010}, who emphasize that bureaucratic effort responds to monitoring.} Aggregate monitoring is the sum of individual contributions, $M=\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i$. Because monitoring by one beneficiary can raise output for all, monitoring is a public good among the principals.

Beneficiary $i$ has utility $u_i(R(e(\sum_{k=1}^n m_k)), w_i-c_i m_i)$, where $w_i$ is the endowment and $c_i$ is the unit cost of monitoring. Since $R$ and $e$ are monotonic transformations of $M$, the first argument is the public good generated by the bureaucracy and the second is the private good left after monitoring costs.

We assume that the public and private goods are perfect substitutes. Public-goods theory treats perfect substitutability as a special case \citep{Varian1992, BatinaIhori2005}. In this setting, the assumption is natural because the public good is monetary or money-like output produced through costly monitoring. A beneficiary is willing to spend up to one dollar of private resources to obtain one dollar of bureaucracy-generated output. If a city fails to collect garbage, for example, a resident is willing to contact the agency or file complaints until the cost of doing so equals the cost of private collection. The same logic applies to other bureaucracy-provided services such as roads, education, policing, and, in our application, lease income.

We solve a one-shot, simultaneous-move contribution game under complete information. The model has two standard implications. With equal stakes and identical costs, the symmetric equilibrium pins down total monitoring and therefore the relationship between group size and output. With unequal stakes, the beneficiary with the highest marginal value of the public good contributes monitoring, up to ties, while the others free-ride \citep{Olson1965, BergstromBlumeVarian1986}. We now derive functional relationships that map these implications to beneficiary stakes. In the empirical setting, these stakes are ownership shares.

Let bureaucratic effort be $e(M)=M/(\gamma+M)$. This function is concave on $[0, \infty)$, ranges from zero to one, and makes $\gamma$ the parameter governing the responsiveness of effort to monitoring. A larger $\gamma$ means that monitoring translates into effort more slowly. We adopt this form because it is parsimonious, delivers closed-form equilibrium expressions, and has the boundary behavior the setting requires: zero effort at zero monitoring, and effort approaching one as monitoring grows without bound. Any increasing, strictly concave effort function with the same boundary conditions yields the same qualitative comparative statics. The specific form pins down the functional relationships we take to the data, including the $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ measure. Let $R_l$ denote the maximum attainable output, determined by factors outside bureaucratic effort. For a police department, for example, solved crimes are bounded by criminal activity. In the leasing setting below, attainable rent is bounded by the tract's agricultural value and the local lease market. Realized output is
\begin{equation} \label{(1)}
    R(M, R_l) = \frac{M}{\gamma + M}R_l.
\end{equation}
The derivations below characterize interior monitoring levels, which deliver the exact expressions used in the empirical mapping. If an unconstrained monitoring expression is negative, the nonnegativity constraint sets the relevant contribution to zero and the same comparative statics hold weakly.

Each beneficiary maximizes her share of bureaucratic output net of monitoring costs.\footnote{The public goods we consider are divisible, so beneficiaries can receive different shares of the output.} We use the quasilinear payoff $\pi_i=r_i-c_i m_i$, where $r_i$ is beneficiary $i$'s share of bureaucratic output, $m_i$ is her monitoring contribution, and $c_i$ is her unit cost of monitoring.

Consider equal shares and identical monitoring costs, and normalize the unit monitoring cost to 1. Before turning to private incentives, we define an efficiency benchmark. A planner who could set aggregate monitoring $M$ directly would choose it to maximize total beneficiary surplus, which is output net of monitoring cost,
\[
    \Pi = R - M = \frac{M}{\gamma + M} R_l - M,
\]
taking the bureaucratic production function, the unit monitoring cost, and the ceiling $R_l$ as given. We call the resulting level constrained efficient: efficient because it maximizes total surplus, and constrained because the only lever is monitoring, not the agency's technology or the ceiling $R_l$. The first-order condition $R_l\gamma/(\gamma + M)^2 = 1$ gives
\begin{equation} \label{(2)}
    M^* = \sqrt{R_l \gamma} - \gamma.
\end{equation}
The benchmark $M^*$ is the yardstick for the equilibrium derived below: because each beneficiary ignores the monitoring benefit she confers on the others, private provision falls short of it whenever the output is shared.

Equation \eqref{(2)} shows that the constrained efficient monitoring benchmark is independent of the number of beneficiaries. The corresponding output is 
\begin{equation} \label{(3)}
    R^*=R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}.
\end{equation}

When monitoring is provided privately by identical beneficiaries, beneficiary $i$ maximizes $\displaystyle \pi_i=\frac{R}{n}-m_i$, subject to $m_i \geq 0$. Since $M$ is the sum of all contributions, $M=m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j$. Substituting for $R$ and $M$ in beneficiary $i$'s objective function gives
\[
\pi_i=\frac{m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j}{\gamma + m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j} \frac{R_l}{n}-m_i.
\]
The first-order condition is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \pi_i}{\partial m_i} = \frac{\gamma}{(\gamma + m_i +\sum_{j\neq i} m_j)^2} \frac{R_l}{n} - 1 = 0. \nonumber
\end{equation}
It implies
\begin{equation}
    m_i + \sum_{j\neq i} m_j = \frac{\sqrt{R_l\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma, \nonumber
\end{equation}
and therefore total monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(4)}
    M = \frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma.
\end{equation}

$M<M^*$ if $n>1$, so monitoring is underprovided relative to the constrained efficient benchmark when there are multiple beneficiaries of bureaucratic output. Monitoring decreases in $n$. When $n=1$, then $M=M^*$. Substituting $M$ from equation \eqref{(4)} into \eqref{(1)} gives realized bureaucratic output under equal shares.
\begin{equation}
    R=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma}{\gamma +\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma} R_l \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(5)}
    R= R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{n}.
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{(5)} predicts that bureaucracy-generated output decreases in the number of beneficiaries when beneficiaries have equal shares and identical costs. The gap between the constrained efficient output $R^*$ and realized output is
\begin{equation}
    R^*-R=(R_l - \sqrt{R_l \gamma})-(R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{n}) \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(6)}
    R^*-R=- \sqrt{R_l\gamma} + \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{n}.
\end{equation}

Now let beneficiaries hold unequal shares of bureaucratic output while retaining equal monitoring costs. The constrained efficient amount of monitoring remains the same as in the equal-share case. Privately, however, beneficiary $i$ maximizes $\pi_i=s_i R-m_i$, where $s_i$ is the beneficiary's output share. The first-order condition analogous to the one above gives the reaction function
\begin{equation} \label{(7)}
    m_i = \sqrt{s_i} \sqrt{R_l \gamma} -\gamma -\sum_{j\neq i}{m_j}.
\end{equation}

Let $\bar m_i=\sqrt{s_i}\sqrt{R_l\gamma}-\gamma$ denote the monitoring contribution beneficiary $i$ would choose if no other beneficiary contributed. Because monitoring contributions cannot be negative, the best response is $m_i=\max\{\bar m_i-\sum_{j\neq i}m_j,0\}$. If one beneficiary has the unique largest share, that beneficiary has the highest $\bar m_i$ and contributes all monitoring in the simultaneous-move, complete-information game. All other beneficiaries free-ride. If several beneficiaries tie for the largest share, the allocation of monitoring among them is not unique, but aggregate monitoring and output are unchanged. In the empirical setting, beneficiaries are owners. We therefore denote the largest ownership share by $s_{max}$.

The total amount of monitoring provided is
\begin{equation} \label{(8)}
    M = \sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}}-\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the output generated by the bureaucracy is
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}}-\gamma}{\gamma + \sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}} - \gamma}R_l \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(9)}
    R = R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent
The output gap is
\begin{equation}
    R^*-R = (R_l -\sqrt{R_l \gamma})-(R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right)) \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(10)}
    R^*-R =-\sqrt{R_l\gamma} + \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right).
\end{equation}
\noindent
The model therefore predicts that bureaucracy-generated output increases with the largest owner's share.

The model can also accommodate beneficiaries who differ in monitoring costs. Appendix~A (``The Organized-Owner Extension'') develops this case for an organized owner, meaning an owner whose organizational form or formal access to the agency lowers its unit cost of monitoring, and the individual owner with the largest ownership share. The equilibrium contributor is the owner with the larger cost-adjusted ownership share, $s_g$ for the organized owner, whose cost is normalized to one, or $s_I/c_I$ for the largest individual owner. An organized owner such as a tribe can therefore contribute all monitoring without holding the largest ownership share if its monitoring cost is sufficiently low. The extension adds the prediction that lower monitoring costs raise monitoring and output.




\section{The BIA and American Indian Trust Land} \label{Background}

\subsection{Allotment, trust status, and fractionation} \label{History}

Allotted trust land matters for this paper because federal law separated beneficial ownership from the ordinary rights to sell, mortgage, or partition land. The General Allotment Act of 1887, commonly called the Dawes Act, authorized the federal government to survey reservation land and issue individual allotments to American Indians. The statutory schedule assigned 160 acres to each head of family, 80 acres to each single adult and orphan child, and 40 acres to other minors, with additional acreage where land was useful only for grazing. After allotments were made, land remaining within a reservation could be declared surplus and opened to non-Indian settlement. Allotment was an assimilation policy that sought to replace tribal landholding with individual farming and individual title. It also transferred large amounts of land out of Indian ownership. Indian landholdings fell from roughly 138 million acres before allotment to roughly 48 million acres by 1934 \citep{Carlson1981,Otis2014,McDonnell1991,DippelFryeLeonard2025}.\footnote{General Allotment Act of 1887, ch. 119, 24 Stat. 388, \S\S 1, 5. The Bureau of Indian Affairs describes over 90 million acres as passing out of Indian ownership between the Dawes Act and 1934 \citep{BIAIndianLandConsolidationHistory}.} The legal structure created by allotment continues to shape modern Indian land tenure \citep{Royster1995,Shoemaker2020}.

Most allotments did not convey unrestricted fee-simple title at the outset. Under section 5 of the Dawes Act, the United States held allotted land in trust for the allottee or the allottee's heirs. The allottee held beneficial ownership, including the right to use the land and receive income from it, but did not hold the full bundle of ordinary private property rights. Trust interests could not be sold, encumbered, mortgaged, or taxed without federal action. The Burke Act of 1906 added an early-exit mechanism. If the Secretary of the Interior judged an allottee competent and capable of managing his or her affairs, the Secretary could issue a fee-simple patent. After a fee patent, the land became taxable and could be sold or encumbered without the same federal restrictions.\footnote{Burke Act of 1906, ch. 2348, 34 Stat. 182.}

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended new allotments and extended the trust periods and restrictions on alienation for remaining trust allotments.\footnote{Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, ch. 576, 48 Stat. 984, codified as amended at 25 U.S.C. \S 5101 et seq. Section 5101 ended new allotment. Section 5102 extended existing trust periods and restrictions on alienation.} This did not ban all land transfers. It did mean that owners generally could not use the ordinary private-market routes by which co-owners consolidate tenancy in common, including unrestricted sale, mortgage, or partition under state property law.

These restrictions interacted with inheritance law. Before 1910, allottees generally could not devise trust allotments by will. Congress authorized wills in 1910, but a will that disposed of trust property had no legal force unless the Secretary approved it.\footnote{Act of June 25, 1910, ch. 431, \S 2, 36 Stat. 856, codified as amended at 25 U.S.C. \S 373.} For much of the twentieth century, if an owner died without an approved will, the owner's interest descended under state or territorial rules incorporated by federal law. Modern probate is governed by federal and approved tribal rules under the Indian Land Consolidation Act and the American Indian Probate Reform Act. Across these regimes, heirs commonly received undivided shares as tenants in common. Each heir received a fractional claim on the whole tract rather than a demarcated physical piece of land. Because consolidation generally required federal approval or a probate-related mechanism, each probate tended to add owners and reduce the size of individual shares \citep{Shoemaker2003,Shoemaker2020,DippelFryeLeonard2025}.

We refer to the resulting dispersion of undivided ownership shares as ownership fractionation. These undivided interests determine both how lease income is divided and each owner's incentive to monitor the BIA. The Supreme Court's description of Tract 1305 illustrates the scale of the problem. The 40-acre tract produced \$1,080 in annual income, had 439 owners, and cost the BIA an estimated \$17,560 per year to administer. One-third of its owners received less than five cents a year in rent, and the smallest heir's share paid a cent every 177 years \citep{HodelIrving1987}. Tract 1305 is extreme but not unique: in our lease records, 24 tracts have at least as many owners. For most of these owners, the rent at stake is too small to repay much effort, although together their claims make up the whole tract's income.

Congress and the Department of the Interior have tried to reduce fractionation while keeping land in trust. These reforms show that transfers are possible, but consolidation generally proceeds through federally supervised mechanisms rather than ordinary private partition or unrestricted sale. The April 2010 ownership snapshot predates congressional approval of the Cobell settlement in December 2010 and the Department of the Interior's establishment of the Land Buy-Back Program in December 2012. The estimates therefore describe fractionation before that program began purchasing fractional interests.\footnote{The Indian Land Consolidation Act of 1983 initially used escheat rules for very small interests, which the Supreme Court invalidated in \citet{HodelIrving1987}. Later reforms relied on probate rules, consolidation agreements, and voluntary acquisition. The American Indian Probate Reform Act of 2004 created federal probate rules for most individually owned trust and restricted land and authorized purchase options at probate for eligible heirs, co-owners, and tribes. Congress approved the Cobell settlement through the Claims Resolution Act of 2010. The settlement provided a \$1.9 billion fund for voluntary purchases of fractional interests that were restored to tribal trust ownership \citep{AIPRA2004,ClaimsResolutionAct2010,DOILandBuyBackProgram2023}.}

\subsection{BIA agricultural leasing} \label{Leasing}

The BIA administers the trust land on which the leases in our data are signed. It maintains land-title and probate records, approves conveyances, and manages trust-land leases. Agricultural leasing of individually owned Indian land is governed by 25 C.F.R. Part 162, Subpart B. Indian landowners may grant agricultural leases, including leases of undivided interests in fractionated tracts, subject to BIA approval. On a fractionated tract, the owners of a majority interest may grant an agricultural lease on behalf of all Indian owners, again subject to BIA approval. The BIA may also grant a lease on behalf of individual owners when owner agreement cannot be assembled and the lease is needed to protect their interests.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.107, 162.207, 162.209. The regulations use grant to describe the act of entering into the lease. Earlier unanimity requirements were relaxed by the American Indian Agricultural Resource Management Act of 1993, Pub. L. No. 103-177, 107 Stat. 2011.}

BIA lease services include contacting owners, advertising or assisting negotiation, determining fair annual rental, approving lease terms, recording the lease, collecting rent when payment runs through the agency, and enforcing lease obligations. When the BIA grants a lease on the owners' behalf, the regulations require it to obtain a fair annual rental and to use an appraisal, competitive bidding, or another appropriate valuation method when needed.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.107, 162.211, 162.222.}

Leases specify how rent is paid. Under agency pay, the lessee pays the BIA, and the agency distributes each owner's share through the trust-account system. Under direct pay, the lease specifies that the lessee pays the landowners directly, subject to BIA approval and documentation requirements.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. \S 162.226.} We use direct payment later as a proxy for owner involvement.

Some allotted tracts include tribal undivided ownership interests. Tribal interests can arise through consolidation, probate, or other federally approved conveyances that move fractional interests into tribal ownership. A tribal co-owner receives a share of the lease income like any other co-owner. Tribal governments also have regular government-to-government channels with the BIA, which may lower the cost of monitoring the agency. This is why tribal ownership is informative for the monitoring-cost prediction developed in the model.

Some tracts also include fee-simple undivided interests. A fee-simple interest can arise when a trust or restricted interest leaves federal supervision, including through a fee patent or removal of restrictions. The interest can remain an undivided share of the same tract. The BIA administers the lease for the trust and restricted interests, and the aggregate trust or restricted share must be identified when the tract also contains fee-simple interests. This distinction matters because the lease-income outcome records rent on the trust and restricted interests that the BIA administers, not rent on the unrestricted fee-simple share.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. Part 162, Subpart B, and 25 C.F.R. \S 152.6. Section 152.6 allows the Secretary to issue a patent in fee for trust land or an interest in trust land acquired by inheritance or devise by a non-Indian or by a person of Indian descent to whom the United States owes no trust responsibility.}

These institutional facts map directly to the model. A lease produces a single tract-level output: annual lease income on the trust portion of the tract. Owners receive that income in proportion to their undivided ownership interests. If one owner contacts the BIA, supplies information about land use, requests a higher rent, or monitors compliance, all co-owners benefit through the same lease income. Monitoring is therefore a public good among the co-owners of a tract. Inheritance-generated fractionation changes the distribution of claims among beneficiaries while leaving the BIA as the common lease administrator for many signed leases. Fractionation can also reduce land use for a different reason: assembling owner consent is harder when a tract has more owners. That consent-cost channel matters most for whether a lease is signed, an extensive margin outside the lease data. The empirical results therefore speak to the intensive margin of lease income per trust acre, not to whether land is leased at all.

\section{Data} \label{Data}

Our data come from the Trust Asset and Accounting Management System, or TAAMS, the Department of the Interior's system of record for title and land-resource management of Indian trust and restricted land \citep{DOITAAMSSORN2014}.\footnote{We received the anonymized administrative extract from a senior officer at the BIA. The version used for analysis does not contain owner names or direct personal identifiers.} TAAMS records land ownership, contracts and leases, beneficial owners, and trust-asset management information. The main analytic extract is an April 2010 snapshot of leases active in 2010 and ownership interests recorded at that date. We focus on agricultural leases signed between 2006 and 2010. In the constructed agricultural sample, 96 percent of leases have terms of five years or less. The restriction keeps the lease date close to the ownership snapshot for most leases. A separate September 28, 2009 ownership snapshot provides a short-run validation of the ownership measures (Section~\ref{Identification} and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ownership_snapshot_validation}). Deaths and probate orders between lease signing and April 2010 can still make the snapshot ownership structure more fractionated than the ownership structure in force when a lease was signed. This is a measurement limitation, not the motivation for the IV design.

The unit of observation is an agricultural lease on a single tract of allotted trust land. We keep leases that cover exactly one tract, so each observation has one ownership structure and one lease outcome.\footnote{Some leases cover several tracts with different ownership structures. Including them would require aggregating ownership across tracts and assigning one contract payment to tracts with different ownership claims. Restricting to one-tract leases avoids those aggregation choices.} For each lease we observe the recorded lease-income field, lease-signing year, lease term, anonymized owner identifiers and undivided ownership interests, reservation, tract acreage, payment arrangement, tribal ownership, fee-simple share, and the resource type conveyed by the lease. The extract does not report tract coordinates, but its parcel identifiers encode each tract's location in the Public Land Survey System, which we use to attach terrain and soil measures for the sensitivity checks in Section~\ref{Identification}.\footnote{The raw parcel identifier encodes Public Land Survey System geography below the reservation. A tract corresponds to one or more of these parcels, so we recover section-, quarter-section-, or aliquot-level parcel locations from the Bureau of Land Management's national PLSS data \citep{BLMNationalPLSS}, assign map units from the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO) \citep{SoilSurveyStaffSSURGO} to sampled points within each parcel, and aggregate parcel values to the tract using acreage weights. In the preferred IV sample, 2,189 of 2,211 tracts can be geolocated. The representative parcel locations used in the matching resolve 1,073 tracts at the section level, 661 at the quarter-section level, and 455 at the aliquot level. The common land-characteristic comparison retains 2,168 leases across 36 reservations after complete-case and fixed-effect processing. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_characteristics_sensitivity} reports the matching details and the resulting sensitivity estimates.}

We construct ownership measures from recorded undivided ownership interests that define the owners of the tract for lease administration. We exclude beneficial-only interests because they do not represent current undivided ownership interests used to administer the lease.\footnote{The excluded TAAMS beneficial-only interest-type codes are C, D, H, I, L, M, Q, R, S, and W.} From the remaining interests, we compute the number of owners, the largest owner's share, the ownership Herfindahl index, the shares held by the largest two, five, and ten owners, an indicator for whether a tribe is the largest owner, and the fee-simple share of the tract.

TAAMS separates lease payments made to the BIA for distribution from lease payments made directly to landowners. We measure direct payment in two ways. The directly paid share is the fraction of a lease's income paid directly to landowners. The direct-payment indicator equals one when any part of the income is paid directly; we call these leases direct-payment leases and the rest agency-pay leases.

The extract covers leased land only and does not include unleased allotted trust tracts that could have been leased, so the estimates are conditional on lease formation.

We then impose two restrictions that align the analysis with the individual-allotment channel in the model. First, we drop wholly tribal tracts. A wholly tribal tract has one owner and is leased through the tribe's government-to-government relationship with the BIA rather than through the individual-owner channel described by the model. We retain tracts in which a tribe holds a partial interest alongside individual owners and use that variation in the descriptive analyses of monitoring costs. We also drop the few leases involving a government or administrative account rather than an individual or tribal owner.

Second, we restrict the analysis to agricultural leases. For agricultural leases, annual lease income per trust acre is a natural measure of the output the BIA secures because lease payments reflect the surface use of land in local agricultural rental markets.\footnote{Among income-generating nonagricultural lease types in the extract, oil and gas leases are next most numerous. They are not comparable on a per-acre lease-income basis because payments depend on reserves, extraction costs, and operator-specific bargaining, not only on surface acreage.} Agricultural leasing falls under 25 C.F.R. Part 162. We do not study grazing permits under Part 166. Grazing land is more fractionated than the agricultural tracts we examine in the extract, so the sample does not select the most fractionated trust-land uses.

The outcome is the natural logarithm of annual lease income per trust acre. We interpret the recorded income field as the annual rental amount paid on the trust or restricted interests that the BIA administers.\footnote{The available extract specification does not define the field's periodicity. The scale of recorded lease income relative to USDA county agricultural rents and the predominance of one fiscal-year payment per agricultural lease document in the audited FY2008 BIA payment ledger support this interpretation.} When a tract also contains fee-simple undivided interests, TAAMS does not record lease payments for the fee-simple portion. We therefore divide recorded income by trust acreage, defined as total tract acreage multiplied by one minus the fee-simple share, so that the numerator and denominator describe the same BIA-administered portion of the tract. The baseline sample drops observations outside the first and ninety-ninth percentiles of income per trust acre. Robustness checks below retain the full positive-income sample and use alternative trimming thresholds.

The resulting constructed sample contains 9,409 leases across 52 reservations. Six reservations contribute only one lease and drop out when we include reservation fixed effects. The regressions therefore use 9,403 leases across 46 reservations in the full sample and 8,662 leases across 45 reservations after dropping sole-owner tracts. The preferred instrumental-variables sample is smaller because it requires multi-owner, surface-resource-only, agency-pay leases with qualifying probate orders on or after 1938 and excludes the Crow Reservation. It contains 2,211 leases on 38 reservations.

Table~\ref{tab:table1} reports summary statistics. Panel A summarizes lease, ownership, and tract variables. Panel B reports the probate-timing instruments in the IV-eligible sample. Appendix Figures~\ref{figA1}--\ref{figA4} show the distributions of lease income per trust acre, the number of owners, the largest ownership share, and the tribal ownership share.

\section{Empirical Strategy} \label{Econometrics}

Empirically, we use the lease data in two ways. The first is a descriptive least-squares exercise that asks whether lease income per trust acre covaries with ownership fractionation in the direction predicted by the model, after conditioning on reservation and lease-signing year. The second is the probate-timing instrumental-variables design that provides the paper's causal estimate. It uses the timing of inheritance events and estate processing to predict ownership fractionation within reservation and lease-signing year. The design does not require probate timing to be random in the raw data. A causal interpretation requires residual probate-order timing, after controls and fixed effects, to be conditionally unrelated to unobserved determinants of lease income and to affect lease income only through ownership fractionation.

The descriptive least-squares baseline is
\begin{equation} \label{(20)}
   y_{ijt} = \beta\, F_{ij} + X_{ijt}'\gamma + \mu_j + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ijt},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $y_{ijt}$ is log annual lease income per trust acre on tract $i$ on reservation $j$ for a lease signed in year $t$, $F_{ij}$ is a fractionation measure, $X_{ijt}$ is a vector of lease characteristics, and $\mu_j$ and $\delta_t$ are reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects. Reservation fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences across reservations in land quality, regulation, agency practice, and local rental markets. Lease-signing year fixed effects absorb common agricultural-rent shocks and production conditions in the year a lease is signed. In the descriptive regressions, $X_{ijt}$ includes lease term, resource-type indicators with surface-resource-only leases as the omitted category, log tract acreage, and the fee-simple share of the tract. In the IV sample every lease is surface-resource-only, so the IV controls are lease term, fee-simple share, and log tract acreage. We cluster standard errors by reservation, the level at which local agency practice, rental-market shocks, and residual correlation in the instrument are likely to operate.

The model motivates both the form of $F_{ij}$ and the sign of $\beta$. The primary measure is the inverse square root of the largest owner's share, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, implied by equation~\eqref{(9)}. The model predicts $\beta<0$, because a larger top share lowers $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ and raises income. We do not tie the result to that transformation alone. We also report the largest share entered linearly and as its inverse, which equals the number of owners when shares are equal; the square root of the owner count, the form implied by equation~\eqref{(5)}, and its logarithm; the combined share of the largest two, five, or ten owners; and the ownership Herfindahl index. All measures have the same economic prediction. Less fractionation should raise lease income per trust acre.

Equation~\eqref{(20)} is not causal. Ownership structure is the product of more than a century of inheritance. Tracts that became heavily fractionated may differ in unobserved land productivity, location within the reservation, original allottee characteristics, or family history. A central concern is bias toward zero. If lower-productivity land made families poorer and left them with fewer surviving heirs, it would be less fractionated for reasons unrelated to monitoring. The same lower productivity would also depress lease income. Least squares would then make fractionation look less harmful because more-fractionated tracts are positively selected on unobserved productivity. The instrumental-variables design targets omitted-variable bias by using residual variation in probate timing.

The IV design uses probate timing. Fractionation accumulates when an owner dies and a probate order divides the owner's undivided ownership interest among heirs. We instrument $F_{ij}$ with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders on the tract, denoted $Z_{ij}=(\underline{P}_{ij},\overline{P}_{ij})$. TAAMS does not contain a separate exact signing date for each lease. A probate order qualifies if it was approved no later than the lease effective date, the consistently recorded date tied to the beginning of the lease term. The first stage is
\begin{equation} \label{(21)}
   F_{ij} = Z_{ij}'\pi + X_{ijt}'\rho + \mu_j + \delta_t + \nu_{ijt}.
\end{equation}
\noindent Estimating equation~\eqref{(20)} by two-stage least squares, with $F_{ij}$ instrumented by $Z_{ij}$, has a causal interpretation under four conditions. Relevance requires probate timing to predict ownership fractionation. Conditional independence requires residual probate-order timing, after reservation fixed effects, lease-signing year fixed effects, and controls, to be uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of lease income. The no-direct-effect condition requires probate timing to affect lease income only through ownership fractionation. Monotonicity for the local interpretation requires changes in each probate-timing instrument to shift fractionation in its first-stage direction for otherwise comparable tracts, rather than reversing direction for a subset of tracts. Figure~\ref{fig:iv_dag} summarizes the intended path and the main threats to these conditions. Section~\ref{Identification} reports the first-stage evidence and diagnostics that probe specific threats to conditional independence and the no-direct-effect condition.

These conditions are not mechanical. Probate-order approval dates combine inheritance events and administrative processing. Family resources or income from the tract may affect mortality. Land endowments and prior fractionation may shape both probate histories and lease income. Local administrative capacity may affect both probate processing and lease administration. The design does not treat deaths or processing delays as random. Because each co-owner receives only a share of a tract's rental income, any direct effect of income from one tract on an owner's resources is attenuated by that share. This consideration narrows, but does not eliminate, the mortality concern and does not address broader family-resource channels.

With heterogeneous effects and a continuous ownership measure, two-stage least squares estimates a weighted average of causal responses for the margins of fractionation shifted by the probate-timing instruments \citep{MogstadTorgovitsky2018}. We therefore interpret the coefficient and the 40-to-20 translation as local to this probate-timing variation, not as global average effects for all allotted trust land. Policy discussions about consolidating fractional interests within trust should read the estimate with the same local qualification.

The IV identifies the total effect of ownership fractionation on lease income under the maintained assumptions. It does not separately identify owner monitoring. The monitoring model organizes the primary interpretation, but the total effect also includes any effect of fractionation through accumulated investment in the land, which the design cannot separate from monitoring. We study three secondary margins descriptively. Tribal co-ownership can lower the cost of contacting the BIA, and direct payment can proxy for greater owner involvement. Fee-simple share captures a different ownership mix and bargaining environment. The probate-timing instruments do not identify the causal effect of any of these margins, so Section~\ref{OtherPredictions} reports them as descriptive associations after the IV estimates.

The preferred IV sample follows the institutional channel in the model: the BIA remains the bargaining and administrative agent for multiple owners. This definition excludes the Crow Reservation. Special federal regulations allow Crow Indians classified as competent to lease trust lands and their minor children's trust lands for farming or grazing without Secretarial approval, subject to statutory limits \citep{ECFR25CFR162600}. Separate regulations govern the competency classification of Crow Indians \citep{ECFR25CFR153}. The extract contains a Crow-competent encumbrance code, but the available documentation does not establish that every competent agricultural lease is consistently separated from ordinary agricultural leases. We therefore cannot reliably classify the full affected set. The preferred sample excludes Crow and contains 2,211 leases on 38 reservations. The main IV exhibits also report Crow-included estimates for comparison, and Appendix~A (``Crow Competency-Leasing Regime and the IV Sample'') gives the regulatory details.

Inference accounts for the small number of reservation clusters. With 38 clusters in the preferred sample, conventional cluster-robust standard errors can over-reject. We therefore report wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values as the primary $p$-values \citep{CameronGelbachMiller2008}. Because two-stage least squares can also be sensitive to weak instruments, we report weak-instrument-robust Anderson--Rubin confidence sets computed with the same bootstrap \citep{AndersonRubin1949}. We also report conditional likelihood ratio confidence sets as a weak-instrument-robust complement. We measure first-stage strength with the effective $F$-statistic of \citet{MontielOleaPflueger2013}. With two instruments for one endogenous regressor, we report a test of the overidentifying restriction. As usual, the test can detect some violations of the joint IV conditions, but it cannot establish either conditional independence or the no-direct-effect condition. The next section presents the results, beginning with descriptive fractionation evidence. Section~\ref{Identification} develops the instrument and reports the two-stage least-squares estimates.

\section{Results} \label{Results}
\subsection{Descriptive ownership patterns}

The least-squares estimates provide the descriptive benchmark for the paper's evidence hierarchy. They ask whether lease income per trust acre is higher on tracts with more concentrated ownership after conditioning on reservation, lease-signing year, and the lease controls. They do not identify the causal effect of fractionation. Unobserved land productivity, original allottee characteristics, location within the reservation, and family history may affect both ownership structure and lease income. Here we focus on the multi-owner sample, where the collective-action problem in monitoring can arise.

Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact} holds the sample and controls fixed and varies only the ownership measure. Column 1 uses the model-implied measure, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, which rises as the largest owner's share falls. The coefficient is $-0.0334$, with a standard error of 0.0104 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.014. Moving from a 40 percent largest-owner share to a 20 percent largest-owner share raises $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.655, so the estimate implies about 0.022 log points lower income, or about 2.2 percent lower lease income per trust acre.

Columns 2, 4, and 5 enter ownership concentration directly. A 10-percentage-point increase in the largest owner's share is associated with about 0.012 log points higher income per trust acre in column 2, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.013. Moving the ownership Herfindahl index from zero to one is associated with 0.130 log points higher income in column 4, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.018. The combined share of the two largest owners gives the same sign and somewhat stronger inference in column 5, where the coefficient is 0.099 and the wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is 0.004. These columns all use concentration measures rather than fractionation measures, so the predicted sign is positive.

Owner count points in the same direction but is less informative in this least-squares comparison. Column 3 scales the count by 1,000 owners. The coefficient is $-0.339$, which implies that 100 additional owners are associated with about 0.034 log points lower income. The wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is 0.100, and the estimate is not statistically distinguishable from zero at the 5 percent level. This imprecision is consistent with the model's emphasis on the largest owner's stake rather than the sheer number of owners. Two tracts can have the same number of owners but different monitoring incentives if ownership shares are distributed differently.

The binned residualized figures show why we treat the largest-owner measure as the preferred descriptive object. Figure~\ref{fig1} plots residualized lease income against the largest owner's share after removing the same fixed effects and controls used in Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact}. The bins rise with the largest owner's share, and a binscatter linearity test does not reject a linear relationship ($p = 0.19$). Figure~\ref{fig2} repeats the exercise for the number of owners, restricting to tracts with no tribal ownership. The relationship is visibly less regular, and a binscatter linearity test rejects linearity ($p = 0.02$). The figures therefore reinforce the table's main pattern. The model predicts a relationship with ownership concentration rather than owner count alone, and the data match that prediction.

These descriptive results are consistent with the monitoring mechanism, but they do not establish causality. The omitted land, allottee, and family characteristics discussed above may shape both ownership and lease income, and Section~\ref{Econometrics} explains why the central concern is bias toward zero. Section~\ref{Identification} therefore instruments fractionation with probate timing to estimate the effect under explicit instrumental-variables assumptions.

\subsection{Probate-timing IV estimates} \label{Identification}

Fractionation is the cumulative record of inheritance. Allotment initially assigned a tract to an individual owner. As that owner and later heirs died, probate orders divided undivided ownership interests among the next generation, and each order could add co-owners \citep{Shoemaker2014}. The 2010 ownership structure therefore reflects both how long a tract has been exposed to inheritance and how recently its interests continued to divide. We summarize that history with two instruments: the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date.

The instruments are relevant because earlier and more extensive inheritance histories produce more fractionated ownership. The identifying variation has two components: when owners died and when the agency processed the resulting estates. The design does not require raw probate timing to be random. A causal interpretation requires residual probate timing, after reservation fixed effects, lease-signing year fixed effects, and controls, to be conditionally unrelated to unobserved determinants of lease income and to have no direct effect on lease income outside its effect through ownership fractionation. The evidence in this section has three roles. The first-stage and reduced-form results document the empirical links from probate timing to fractionation and lease income. The main IV table reports the estimate under the maintained IV assumptions. The timing, finer-geography, cohort, land-characteristic, and record-completeness exercises probe specific threats to those assumptions. Alternative ownership measures and leave-one-reservation-out checks assess functional-form robustness and influence.

Table~\ref{tab:iv_firststage} shows that the first stage is strong in the preferred Crow-excluded sample. Regressing the main fractionation measure on the two probate-timing instruments yields an effective $F$-statistic of 75.7 and a first-stage partial $R^2$ of 0.096. The signs have the expected interpretation. A later earliest qualifying probate order predicts less fractionation, with a coefficient of $-0.0062$ and a standard error of 0.0007. A later latest qualifying probate order predicts more fractionation, with a coefficient of 0.0167 and a standard error of 0.0020. The reduced-form coefficients move lease income in the corresponding direction. A later earliest order predicts higher lease income, with a coefficient of 0.0014 and a standard error of 0.0009. A later latest order predicts lower lease income, with a coefficient of $-0.0039$ and a standard error of 0.0016.

Figure~\ref{fig:iv_fs_rf} shows the same first-stage and reduced-form relationships using a standardized probate-timing index. Panel A plots the first stage. A one-standard-deviation increase in the index raises residualized $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.200, with a standard error of 0.020. Panel B plots the reduced form. The same index lowers residualized log lease income per trust acre by 0.0469, with a standard error of 0.0140. The ratio of these two slopes is the two-stage least-squares coefficient for the index specification. The figure therefore makes transparent why the IV estimate is negative and larger in magnitude than the least-squares association.

The main IV estimate shows that fractionation lowers lease income per trust acre. In Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main}, the preferred row instruments the model-implied ownership measure, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders. The two-stage least-squares coefficient is $-0.234$, with a standard error of 0.070 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.020. The Anderson--Rubin 95 percent confidence interval runs from $-0.415$ to $-0.066$, and the conditional likelihood ratio 95 percent confidence set, reported in the table notes, runs from $-0.437$ to $-0.067$. Moving from a largest-owner share of 40 percent to 20 percent raises $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.655. The estimate therefore implies a decline of 0.153 log points, or about 14 percent, in lease income per trust acre.

The same-sample least-squares comparison in Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} is much smaller. In the IV sample, the least-squares coefficient on $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ is $-0.037$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.264. The corresponding 40-to-20 translation is about 2.4 percent and is not statistically distinguishable from zero. The IV and least-squares estimates do not estimate the same object. The IV coefficient is local to tracts whose ownership structure responds to probate timing, while least squares summarizes the conditional association in the observed IV sample. Still, the gap between the two estimates is consistent with the omitted-quality channel described above. If lower-productivity land both lowers lease income and leaves poorer families with fewer surviving heirs, less-fractionated tracts look low-income for reasons unrelated to monitoring, and least squares is biased toward zero.

The remaining rows of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} show that the sign is not tied to one transformation. Each row uses the same two probate-timing instruments and changes the endogenous ownership measure. Measures that increase with concentration, such as the largest owner's share, have positive coefficients. Measures that increase with fractionation, such as the inverse largest-owner share and log number of owners, have negative coefficients. The log-owner row gives an intuitive scale: the coefficient is $-0.0569$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.020, so doubling the owner count lowers lease income per trust acre by about 4 percent in the preferred IV sample. These alternatives are robustness checks, not competing preferred specifications. The preferred measure remains $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ because the model predicts that monitoring incentives depend on the stake of the owner with the strongest private incentive to monitor. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:count_iv_robustness} reports row-specific first-stage diagnostics for the count-based alternatives. Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} also reports two broader concentration measures. The cumulative share of the top five owners has a coefficient of 0.440, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.020, and the ownership Herfindahl index has a coefficient of 0.547, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.034.

The Crow-included columns of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} show how much the institutional sample definition matters. Including Crow gives a coefficient of $-0.176$, with a standard error of 0.073 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.048. The Anderson--Rubin interval runs from $-0.344$ to 0.016, so the weak-instrument-robust interval marginally includes zero. The point estimate moves toward zero by roughly one quarter, consistent with the competency-leasing regime weakening or removing the BIA bargaining channel for some Crow leases (Section~\ref{Econometrics}).

Conditional independence remains a central identifying assumption. Reservation fixed effects remove broad differences in allotment era, climate, terrain, agency practice, and rental markets across reservations. They also remove the variation driven by the fact that a reservation opened to allotment in the 1880s began dividing tracts decades before one opened in 1910. Lease-signing year fixed effects remove common rent conditions, commodity-price movements, and production conditions in the year the lease was signed. This matters especially for the latest-probate instrument because a death shortly before a lease begins can move the latest probate date and the lease date together. After these fixed effects and controls, the design compares tracts on the same reservation and in the same lease-signing year whose family deaths and estate processing occurred at different times. These comparisons reduce variation from observed market and administrative conditions, but they do not establish conditional independence. The no-direct-effect condition is distinct. It would fail if probate timing changed BIA attention or lease administration through a channel other than ownership fractionation.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:instrument_timing} checks whether the instruments are predetermined relative to the lease by comparing the preferred exact-date rule, which uses probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date, with adjacent constructions. A year-level construction that uses probate-order years no later than the lease year gives a similar coefficient, as does a stricter prior-year construction. The latest-through-2010 construction gives a similar point estimate, but it can use probate orders approved after the lease term begins and is therefore not the preferred design. The earliest-only construction drops the latest-probate instrument, the component most exposed to deaths close to the lease date. That just-identified estimate remains negative, at $-0.222$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.041 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.505$ to $-0.003$. These checks support the lease-effective-date construction without replacing it.

Longer lags reduce the role of probate orders processed close to the lease (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel A). Requiring both instruments to precede the lease by at least three, five, or ten years yields coefficients of $-0.218$, $-0.201$, and $-0.174$. These estimates imply 40-to-20 effects of 13.3, 12.3, and 10.8 percent. Each first stage remains strong, each wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is below 0.05, and each Anderson--Rubin and conditional likelihood ratio confidence set excludes zero. The estimates attenuate as the lag increases but remain negative and economically material. The exercise reduces concern about orders processed close to lease start, but it does not make mortality or processing delays exogenous.

Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} then separates inheritance events from later administrative paperwork. A probate order creates new heirs and divides an ownership interest. A probate modification revises an existing order without necessarily adding owners. The main instruments use probate orders only. When we rebuild the instruments using both orders and modifications, the coefficient is $-0.228$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.005 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.361$ to $-0.095$. The result therefore does not come from later paperwork revisions.

A second threat is that probate timing may proxy for allotment cohort within a reservation. Cohorts allotted at different times could differ in soil quality, allotment layout, or regulatory history. Reservation fixed effects do not by themselves remove such within-reservation cohort differences. We therefore add reservation-by-allotment-decade fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust}), using the decade of the earliest recorded conveyance as a proxy because TAAMS does not record pre-1912 trust-patent dates. This specification compares tracts assigned in the same decade on the same reservation. The coefficient remains negative, at $-0.191$, compared with $-0.234$ in the baseline. Precision falls, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.140 and 34 reservation clusters. We read this specification as showing that the point estimate is not mechanically generated by broad allotment-cohort differences, while also recognizing that the more demanding fixed effects reduce power.

Stronger local fixed effects do not attenuate the estimate (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel B). On a common 2,191-lease sample, replacing additive reservation and signing-year fixed effects with reservation-by-signing-year fixed effects moves the coefficient from $-0.223$ to $-0.246$. On a common 2,093-lease sample, adding county-by-signing-year fixed effects alongside reservation fixed effects moves the coefficient from $-0.229$ to $-0.272$. Both stronger specifications retain strong first stages and weak-instrument-robust confidence sets that exclude zero. These variants absorb time-varying reservation or county conditions, including some variation in local administrative capacity and rental markets. Parsed county is not an exact tract boundary, so the estimates remain targeted diagnostics rather than a new preferred specification.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_characteristics_sensitivity} separates basic land endowments from measures that can embed land-use history and reports instrument balance on both. On a common sample of 2,168 leases across 36 reservations, the specification without added land controls gives a coefficient of $-0.233$. Adding slope, elevation, surface sand, and surface clay changes the coefficient to $-0.225$, a 3.5 percent attenuation, and implies a 40-to-20 effect of 13.7 percent. The wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is 0.009. This result materially reduces the narrow concern that these basic, plausibly predetermined endowments explain the preferred estimate. Adding four soil productivity and condition measures instead changes the coefficient to $-0.141$, which implies 8.8 percent and has a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.063. The first stage is essentially unchanged, while the reduced form attenuates. Because these soil measures can reflect broader productive capacity, accumulated management, or investment, the soil result is an ambiguous sensitivity estimate rather than a clean test of conditional independence. We retain the full 2,211-observation specification as preferred because the land-data comparison uses a smaller common sample and the soil measures may partly absorb a causal channel. We do not interpret 8.8 percent as a lower bound.

A second ownership snapshot provides a short-run measurement check (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ownership_snapshot_validation}). The September 2009 file links to 99.95 percent of tracts in the preferred sample. Across linked tracts, correlations between the September 2009 and April 2010 ownership measures exceed 0.99, and the top owner is unchanged for 94.7 percent of tracts. On the identical sample of 1,899 leases effective by September 28, 2009, the IV coefficient is $-0.1999$ when ownership is measured in September 2009 and $-0.1992$ when it is measured in April 2010. The coefficients differ by 0.31 percent, and the 40-to-20 translations differ by 0.036 percentage points. This comparison supports short-run stability of the ownership measure. It does not eliminate measurement error for leases signed earlier in the study window, and the April 2010 measure remains the preferred measure.

Two sample splits check whether measurement drives the estimate (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel C). Leases signed in 2006--2008 are furthest from the April 2010 ownership snapshot, and reservations differ in how completely their probate records are recorded. The coefficient is $-0.352$ for leases signed from 2006 through 2008 and $-0.007$ for leases signed in 2009 or 2010. It is $-0.294$ in reservations below the pre-specified record-completeness median and $-0.141$ in reservations above it, where it is imprecise, with a standard error of 0.126 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.315. In both splits the first-stage coefficients are similar across groups, and the difference comes from the reduced form. The splits do not yield a clear interpretation: the pattern may reflect effect heterogeneity or greater sensitivity where records are less complete, and the interaction estimates are not statistically resolved.

The main estimate also survives changes to the probate window, sample restrictions, and outcome trimming. Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} shows that removing the probate-date eligibility cutoff leaves the coefficient at $-0.235$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.012. Adding the direct-payment leases back gives $-0.232$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.010. Keeping the full positive-income sample without the 1/99 outcome trim gives $-0.227$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.016. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_outlier_appendix} reports additional trimming variants, and the estimates remain negative under the 2/98 and 5/95 trims. Figure~\ref{fig:iv_spec_curve} summarizes these checks and the corresponding Crow-included comparisons. Dropping each reservation one at a time gives estimates between $-0.278$ and $-0.189$, and all 38 are statistically significant at the 5 percent level under conventional cluster-robust inference.

Taken together, the diagnostics support the preferred probate-timing design, but they do not establish conditional independence or the no-direct-effect condition. The evidence combines a strong first stage, reduced-form signs consistent with the model, a stable negative two-stage least-squares estimate, robustness to timing and sample definitions, and no single reservation driving the result. Remaining threats include residual family histories, record quality, unobserved tract attributes, and direct administrative channels linking probate processing to lease administration. The interpretation should therefore remain local to the preferred IV sample and conditional on the maintained IV assumptions.

The population is correspondingly narrow. The broader lease records are close to a census of multi-owner, surface-resource-only agricultural leasing of allotted trust land in the contiguous United States. The preferred IV estimate applies to agency-pay, multi-owner, surface-resource-only leases with post-1938 qualifying probate histories on the 38 non-Crow reservations in the preferred sample. These reservations lie in 11 states, and Montana, the Dakotas, and Oklahoma account for 91 percent of the leases. Most of the leases are on reservations that \citet{Dippel2014} classifies as forced-coexistence reservations; only 75 leases, on 3 reservations, are on reservations he classifies otherwise, too few to test whether the effect depends on that history. The estimate does not extend to Crow leases covered by the competency-leasing regime, wholly tribal land, land that was never allotted, unleased land, grazing permits, or oil and gas leases. The mechanism is not specific to trust land, but the magnitude estimated here need not carry over to other settings.

\subsection{Owner count and free-riding} \label{Mechanism}

The model attributes the income loss to free-riding: as ownership divides among more heirs, each owner captures a smaller share of the return to monitoring. Under equal shares, equation~\eqref{(5)} states this prediction in terms of the number of owners, and the owner-count rows of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} have the predicted sign. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_owner_count} asks where in the ownership distribution the effect arises by splitting the preferred IV sample at 30 owners, into 1,276 leases with 2 to 30 owners and 929 leases with more.

The effect is largest among tracts with 2 to 30 owners. In that range, the coefficient on the log owner count is $-0.174$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.009 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.283$ to $-0.090$, so doubling the owner count lowers lease income per trust acre by about 11 percent. Above 30 owners the estimate is $-0.024$, with an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.323$ to 0.253. Because that interval contains the estimate for 2 to 30 owners, the contrast is suggestive rather than a statistically established difference. The square root of the owner count gives the same pattern, and so does a least-squares spline in the multi-owner sample, whose slope per owner is about 9 times as steep below 30 owners as above; the difference in slopes is imprecise, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.097. The concentration is also consistent with the imprecise linear owner count in Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact}, which gives the most weight to the most fractionated tracts, where an additional owner matters least.

The same split using $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ points in the same direction, but within 2 to 30 owners the two probate instruments disagree: the over-identification test rejects, with a $p$-value of 0.028, and the Anderson--Rubin set is empty. We therefore state the mechanism in owner-count terms. All of these estimates use the same instruments and the same reduced form, so the alternative measures rescale one causal relationship rather than adding independent evidence, and none of them observes monitoring directly.

A descriptive check addresses a different explanation, divided attention. Many owners also hold interests in other agricultural tracts on the same reservation, and an owner spread across many tracts might attend less to each. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:divided_attention} holds within-tract fractionation fixed and relates lease income to how many other tracts the tract's owners hold. Owners' holdings elsewhere are not associated with significantly lower income, whether measured for all owners, for the largest owner, who is the would-be monitor in the model, or for the other owners. The evidence ties the income loss to division within the tract rather than to owners' commitments elsewhere, as the free-riding mechanism predicts.

\subsection{Monitoring costs and owner involvement} \label{OtherPredictions}

Under the maintained IV assumptions, the probate-timing estimates provide the paper's causal evidence on ownership fractionation. This subsection studies three secondary margins descriptively: whether the tribe is the largest owner, whether lease payments are made directly to owners, and the fee-simple share of the tract. The data do not observe owner monitoring actions or BIA responses. The question is whether these associations line up with lower monitoring costs or greater owner involvement.

Whether the tribe is the largest owner is the most direct proxy for lower monitoring costs. Tribes have formal government-to-government channels with the BIA and may be better positioned than individual co-owners to monitor the agency. In the organized-owner extension (Section~\ref{Theory} and Appendix~A), such an owner can supply all monitoring without holding the largest share if its monitoring cost is low enough. Table~\ref{tab:table4} relates log lease income per trust acre to an indicator for whether the tribe is the largest owner. Columns 1 and 2 include reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects and then add fee-simple share. Column 3 adds the lease controls. Column 4 repeats the richer specification in the multi-owner sample. The coefficient ranges from 0.062 to 0.088 across columns, with wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values between 0.010 and 0.027. The richer full-sample estimate in column 3 implies about 9 percent higher lease income per trust acre when the tribe is the largest owner.

Figure~\ref{fig3} shows the same pattern in binned residualized data for tracts with positive tribal ownership. Lease income rises with tribal share, and a binscatter linearity test does not reject a linear relationship at conventional levels ($p=0.07$). The figure is useful because it shows that the Table~\ref{tab:table4} association is not driven only by the binary largest-owner threshold. The appendix checks show that the largest-owner margin carries more information than tribal presence alone.\footnote{Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table5} separates tribal share, largest-owner status, and their interaction. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table_play1} replaces the largest-owner indicator with an indicator for any tribal ownership, which is small and not statistically distinguishable from zero in the richest specification. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table_play4} reports tribal-share functional forms on tracts with positive tribal ownership. The linear tribal-share association is positive, while the inverse-square-root form is not.}

Direct payment is the second proxy for owner involvement. Table~\ref{tab:table8} reports regressions using either the directly paid share or the direct-payment indicator, both defined in Section~\ref{Data}. Column 1 uses the directly paid share in the full sample. A full-range increase in the directly paid share is associated with 0.220 log points higher lease income per trust acre, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.031. Equivalently, a 10-percentage-point larger directly paid share is associated with about 0.022 log points higher income. Column 2 replaces the continuous measure with a direct-payment indicator. The coefficient falls to 0.148, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.103. The continuous measure is therefore more informative than the binary indicator, which groups leases with small directly paid shares together with leases paid almost entirely to owners.

Columns 3 and 4 show that the direct-payment association remains positive in more relevant subsamples. Excluding Crow raises the continuous estimate to 0.256, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.002. Restricting to the multi-owner sample gives 0.251, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.068. These magnitudes are consistent with the owner-involvement interpretation.

Direct payment is nevertheless hard to interpret as a treatment. First, it is an indirect measure of involvement: the BIA approves the lease under either payment arrangement, and the data do not record who negotiated the lease. Second, Figure~\ref{fig4} shows that direct-payment leases are less fractionated than agency-pay leases. The median direct-payment lease has seven owners, compared with 17 owners for agency-pay leases, so direct payment combines owner involvement with the ownership structure studied above. Third, many directly paid rents appear as round hundred-dollar amounts, which suggests coarser recording than for agency-paid rents. For these reasons, we do not instrument direct payment and do not treat the direct-payment coefficient as a clean test of the monitoring channel.

Fee-simple share is the least direct of the three descriptive margins, and its link to trust income runs through the lessee. The BIA does not lease fee interests or collect rent for fee owners, and it does not condition approval of a lease of the trust interests on a lease from the fee owners.\footnote{25 C.F.R. \S 162.102(c) (2011), the rule in force when the sample leases were signed. The current rule, 25 C.F.R. \S 162.004(a), adds that the lessee is responsible for accounting to the owners of any fee interests.} Because every interest is an undivided share of the whole tract, a lessee who farms the tract must also deal with the fee owners, on terms set outside the BIA records. Fee owners hold unrestricted title and negotiate for themselves, so a larger fee-simple share brings owners with a direct stake in the terms into the same transaction. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fee_simple_appendix} relates log lease income per trust acre to the fee-simple share of the tract. Across the richer specifications, a 10-percentage-point larger fee-simple share is associated with roughly 0.027 to 0.030 log points higher lease income per trust acre, with wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values close to 0.06. The positive sign is consistent with direct owner involvement raising the rent on the whole tract, but the data do not record the fee owners' terms, so the association cannot separate that channel from other differences between tracts with and without fee interests.

Taken together, the tribal largest-owner and direct-payment results provide the clearest descriptive evidence on monitoring costs and owner involvement. The fee-simple association points in the same direction but is less directly tied to the model. These patterns do not change the source of identification. The causal estimate remains the probate-timing IV estimate of the effect of ownership fractionation on lease income per trust acre.

\subsection{Magnitude and calibration} \label{Counterfactual}

The main causal magnitude is the 40-to-20 largest-owner-share effect: under the preferred probate-timing IV design, moving from a 40 percent largest-owner share to a 20 percent share lowers lease income per trust acre by about 14 percent. Two supplementary exercises put the estimates on a dollar scale. The first applies the preferred IV design to lease income measured in dollars. The second uses a no-controls levels regression to map the model into monitoring and output gaps. The second exercise is descriptive and does not provide separate causal identification.

Table~\ref{tab:calibration_crowex} reports the model-based calibration. The Crow-excluded column uses 8,387 leases and matches the preferred institutional population, but it is not the preferred IV sample. The Crow-included column is a comparison. The competitive ceiling $R_l$ is lease income per trust acre when bureaucratic effort no longer constrains the lease.\footnote{We mean the competitive lease income attainable on the reservation. Federal restrictions and tribal regulation can create a wedge between on- and off-reservation rents.} We estimate this ceiling using the mean income on single-owner leases negotiated independently of the BIA and the intercept from the model-implied levels regression
\begin{equation} \label{(24)}
    income_{ijt}=\beta_0+\beta_1 \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{\max}}}\right)+\varepsilon_{ijt},
\end{equation}
\noindent The single-owner, independently negotiated leases average \$25.73 per trust acre. The Crow-excluded intercept is \$25.44, and the Crow-included intercept is \$24.76. In the Crow-excluded calibration, the slope is $-1.22$ and the implied responsiveness parameter is $\gamma=0.058$. The constrained efficient monitoring level is \$1.16 per trust acre, or about \$141 per lease-year. Evaluated at the mean largest-owner share, actual monitoring is \$0.72 per trust acre, or about 62 percent of the constrained efficient level. The associated output gap is \$0.69 per trust acre, 3.0 percent of mean lease income, or roughly \$85 per lease-year. We treat this as a lower benchmark.

The preferred dollar-outcome IV implies a model gap of about \$260 per lease-year when evaluated at the mean largest-owner share and about \$390 when averaged across leases. Applying the preferred log-IV estimate to observed lease income and trust acreage gives a larger range, roughly \$430 to \$670 per lease-year, depending on the evaluation convention. These figures compare observed ownership with the constrained efficient benchmark; they are not the dollar value of the 40-to-20 contrast.

Figure~\ref{fig6} plots the model-implied output gap from this calibration against the largest-owner share. The gap falls as the largest-owner share rises because a larger stake raises the private return to monitoring. The solid curve uses the Crow-excluded calibration, and the dashed curve reports the Crow-included comparison.

\section{Conclusion}

Beneficiary monitoring can shape bureaucratic output when one agency decision produces a shared return. Each beneficiary bears the cost of contacting the agency, supplying information, requesting better terms, or monitoring compliance, but receives only a share of the gain. The model shows that dispersed beneficiary stakes weaken private monitoring incentives and lower the output a bureaucracy secures.

We study this problem in agricultural leases for allotted trust land administered by the BIA. Ownership fractionation is the product of more than a century of inheritance, so least-squares estimates may reflect land productivity, original allottee characteristics, or family history. The preferred IV design instruments fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. Under the maintained IV assumptions, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers lease income per trust acre by about 14 percent in the Crow-excluded sample. This is the paper's main causal magnitude and is local to the ownership margins shifted by probate timing. The effect is largest among tracts with 2 to 30 owners, and owners' holdings on other tracts do not account for it.

Targeted diagnostics support this design. The estimate remains negative when probate orders must precede the lease by three, five, or ten years, when the specification absorbs reservation-by-year or county-by-year conditions, and when it controls for basic terrain and surface soil texture. Ownership measures are also stable between September 2009 and April 2010. Section~\ref{Identification} discusses the remaining threats, including the attenuation when soil productivity measures are added. Because the data do not observe owner monitoring or BIA responses, the IV identifies the total effect of fractionation on lease income, including any effect through accumulated investment, rather than a separate monitoring coefficient.

The mechanism points to two classes of institutional responses. Consolidating fractional interests into tribal or fewer individual hands can raise the private return to monitoring. A body that represents the co-owners in their dealings with the BIA can instead aggregate monitoring without changing every ownership share. The BIA already acts for the owners as trustee, but it is the agent whose effort the owners monitor, so it cannot also be the body that monitors on their behalf. The paper does not estimate the effect of either reform. The mechanism points toward ownership concentration or representation within trust, not fee-simple conversion. Fee conversion changes control and ownership rights, and allotment-era conversion out of trust produced large-scale loss of American Indian land.

The estimates come from agricultural leasing of allotted trust land, and Section~\ref{Identification} sets out where they apply. The mechanism is more general. Whenever one agency decision produces a shared return, dividing the claims also divides the incentive to watch the agency. On allotted trust land, generations of inheritance have done exactly that.

\subsection*{The Organized-Owner Extension}
\setcounter{equation}{0}
\renewcommand{\theequation}{A\arabic{equation}}
\renewcommand{\theHequation}{A\arabic{equation}}

An organized owner is an owner whose organizational form or formal access to the agency lowers the unit cost of monitoring. In the application, a tribal owner is the leading example. This appendix extends the theoretical model by allowing owners to differ in monitoring costs as well as in output shares. Section~\ref{Theory} shows that, with common monitoring costs, the largest ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring in equilibrium. With unequal costs, the relevant object is the cost-adjusted ownership share.

We consider two owners who may contribute monitoring: an organized owner $g$ and an individual owner $I$, the individual with the largest ownership share. Other individual owners have weakly smaller shares and the same unit monitoring cost as owner $I$, so none has a larger cost-adjusted ownership share than $I$. It is therefore sufficient to compare owner $g$ with owner $I$. Aggregate monitoring is $M=m_g+m_I$.

Normalize the organized owner's unit cost of monitoring to 1, and let owner $I$'s unit cost be $c_I\geq 1$. The organized owner's payoff is $\pi_g=s_gR-m_g$, where $s_g$ is its output share and $m_g$ is its monitoring contribution. Owner $I$'s payoff is $\pi_I=s_I R-c_I m_I$, where $s_I$ is that owner's output share and $m_I$ is that owner's monitoring contribution.

For any owner $k$ with output share $s_k$ and unit monitoring cost $c_k$, the monitoring level it would choose if the other owner contributed zero is $\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_k/c_k}-\gamma$. The object $s_k/c_k$ is the cost-adjusted ownership share. The best response for owner $I$ is
\begin{equation} \label{(11)}
    m_I = \max\left\{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma-m_g,0\right\},
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the best response for the organized owner is
\begin{equation} \label{(12)}
    m_g = \max\left\{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma-m_I,0\right\}.
\end{equation}

The equilibrium depends on the comparison between $s_g$ and $s_I/c_I$. If $s_g>s_I/c_I$, the organized owner has the larger cost-adjusted ownership share. In equilibrium, the organized owner contributes all monitoring and owner $I$ contributes zero. Aggregate monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(13)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
and output is
\begin{equation} \label{(14)}
   R=R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_g}}\right).
\end{equation}
The constrained efficient benchmark remains $M^* = \sqrt{R_l \gamma} - \gamma$. Because the organized owner has the lowest unit monitoring cost, a planner assigns all monitoring to that owner. The output gap is therefore
\begin{equation} \label{(15)}
    R^*-R = -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}+\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_g}}\right).
\end{equation}

If $s_I/c_I>s_g$, owner $I$ has the larger cost-adjusted ownership share. In equilibrium, owner $I$ contributes all monitoring and the organized owner contributes zero. Aggregate monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(16)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
output is
\begin{equation} \label{(17)}
   R=R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{c_I}{s_I}},
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the output gap is
\begin{equation} \label{(18)}
   R^*-R = -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}+\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{c_I}{s_I}}.
\end{equation}

If $s_g=s_I/c_I$, the two owners have the same cost-adjusted ownership share. The equilibrium allocation of monitoring between them is not unique. Any nonnegative split between $m_g$ and $m_I$ that delivers total monitoring
\begin{equation} \label{(19)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma = \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma
\end{equation}
\noindent
is an equilibrium and yields the same output.

The extension preserves the public-goods logic of the main model. With equal costs, the largest ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring in equilibrium. With unequal costs, the cost-adjusted ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring. Monitoring and output rise with that owner's ownership share and fall with that owner's unit monitoring cost. A lower monitoring cost can make an organized owner contribute all monitoring even when its ownership share is below that of the largest individual owner.

\subsection*{Crow Competency-Leasing Regime and the IV Sample}

Section~\ref{Econometrics} explains why the preferred IV sample excludes the Crow Reservation. Under 25 C.F.R. \S~162.600, Crow Indians classified as competent may lease their trust lands and their minor children's trust lands for farming or grazing without Secretarial approval. The same regulation allows competent Crow Indians to elect Secretarial assistance and requires Secretarial approval for leases signed by Crow Indians not classified as competent and for certain inherited or devised trust lands. Part 153 governs the procedures for determining competency of Crow Indians \citep{ECFR25CFR162600,ECFR25CFR153}.
