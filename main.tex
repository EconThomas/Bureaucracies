\begin{abstract}\noindent
We study how the distribution of stakes among the beneficiaries of a shared agent affects the output the agent secures. In the model, monitoring the agent is a public good among the beneficiaries. With unequal stakes and identical monitoring costs, the beneficiary with the largest stake supplies the monitoring, so output rises with that stake. We test this implication with administrative records on agricultural leases of co-owned allotted trust land administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA). The owners divide the rent that the BIA secures in proportion to their inherited interests, and the timing of inheritance shifts the distribution of their stakes. Because unobserved land productivity, original allottee characteristics, and family histories may affect both ownership fractionation and lease income, we instrument fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. In the preferred instrumental-variables specification, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers lease income per trust acre by 8.7 percent, compared with 2.0 percent in the least-squares estimate. Descriptive patterns in direct payment are consistent with the monitoring interpretation. Dispersed stakes reduce the output a shared bureaucratic agent secures.

\section{Introduction}

On allotted Indian trust land, a single agricultural tract can have hundreds of owners. In the lease records we study, the median co-owned tract has 18 owners, 730 tracts have more than 100, and one has 1,248. The owners lease the tract to a farmer or rancher, and they divide the rent that the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) secures for the tract in proportion to their inherited interests.

This paper studies how the distribution of stakes among the beneficiaries of a shared agent affects the output the agent secures. Bureaucrats often have discretion over how much effort to devote to individual claims, services, and enforcement tasks. Political principals provide broad oversight, but the people most affected by an agency's decisions often monitor those decisions directly by attending hearings, contacting the agency, filing complaints, or writing to decision-makers \citep{Wilson1989}. Monitoring is costly. When one bureaucratic decision produces a shared output, each beneficiary captures only part of the gain from monitoring and has reason to leave that monitoring to others. A group of beneficiaries is privileged, in the term of \citet{Olson1965}, when one member's stake is large enough that this member gains from providing the collective good alone. We formalize this logic as a public-goods problem in monitoring. With equal stakes, more beneficiaries reduce monitoring and output. With unequal stakes and identical monitoring costs, the beneficiary with the largest stake supplies the monitoring, up to ties, so output rises with the largest stake. Using variation in the timing of inheritance, we estimate that reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers lease income per trust acre by 8.7 percent.

We study this question with administrative records on agricultural leases for co-owned allotted trust land administered by the BIA. Inheritance has divided many tracts into undivided ownership interests, meaning fractional claims to the whole tract rather than demarcated physical pieces. Owners receive lease income in proportion to these inherited interests, so they are the beneficiaries in the model, and the BIA is their shared agent. We call the dispersion of their ownership shares ownership fractionation. When an owner contacts the BIA, supplies information about land use, presses for a higher rent when the tract is re-let, or monitors compliance, all owners benefit through the same tract-level lease income. Lease income per trust acre is a market outcome. It measures the output the BIA secures for owners on the trust portion of the tract, while ownership shares determine owners' incentives to monitor the agency. Lease income is observed only for signed leases, so we study this intensive margin, the income a lease secures, not the extensive margin of whether a tract is leased at all.

Ownership structure is endogenous. Unobserved land productivity, original allottee characteristics, and family histories may affect both the ownership structure a tract inherits and the lease income it commands. Our main design instruments ownership fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. Probate orders divide a deceased owner's undivided interest among heirs, so their timing predicts ownership structure. Conditional on reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects, the design compares tracts in the same reservation rental market whose probate histories unfolded at different times. A causal interpretation requires that the residual timing variation be conditionally unrelated to unobserved determinants of lease income and affect income only through ownership fractionation. We probe threats to conditional independence and the no-direct-effect condition with lag-restricted instruments, finer local fixed effects, fixed effects for the decade of a tract's earliest recorded conveyance, and area-weighted terrain and soil measures. We also bound how sensitive the estimate is to a direct effect of probate timing and to selection on unmeasured land quality. We separately assess ownership measurement with a second snapshot, use weak-instrument-robust inference, and report leave-one-reservation-out checks. The preferred IV sample excludes the Crow Reservation because its competency-leasing regime can place some agricultural leases outside the BIA bargaining channel central to the model. Including Crow lowers the main estimate by 30 percent, to a value that is not statistically significant at the 5 percent level.

The main instrumental-variables estimate shows that ownership fractionation lowers lease income per trust acre. In the preferred specification, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers income by 8.7 percent, and by 6.0 percent conditional on measured soil quality. The corresponding least-squares estimate implies a decline of 2.0 percent. Measurement error in recorded ownership shares would bias least squares toward zero, and the IV estimate is local to tracts whose ownership structure responds to probate timing. Both are likely sources of the gap between the two estimates, which the data cannot decompose. Alternative IV specifications using the same probate-timing instruments give the same qualitative pattern when the endogenous ownership measure is the largest owner's share, inverse largest-owner share, the square root and the log of the number of owners, the cumulative share of the largest five owners, or the ownership Herfindahl index.

Additional evidence is consistent with the monitoring interpretation. The co-owners are dispersed principals who share the return from overseeing the BIA. Least-squares estimates show higher lease income when ownership is more concentrated, and the descriptive association for direct payment is consistent with greater owner involvement. Owners' holdings on other agricultural tracts are not associated with lower income, which points to free-riding within the tract rather than attention divided across tracts. Coordination at the majority threshold for consent does not explain the effect either. Where a single owner holds the majority interest required to grant a lease alone, neither lease income nor the share of tracts under lease rises. Because the data do not record monitoring itself, the IV estimate is the total effect of fractionation. That total effect can also include lower accumulated investment, because each co-owner would capture only a share of the return to improving the land, and less effort by the BIA on tracts that are costlier to administer because they have more owners.

To scale the estimates, we use the model to compare fractionated ownership with a benchmark in which the same tract is monitored as if the ownership public-good problem were absent. Applying the preferred IV design to lease income measured in dollars gives an implied income gap of 9.3 to 12.9 percent, or \$264 to \$367 per lease-year, depending on whether the gap is evaluated at the mean largest-owner share or averaged across leases. Translating the preferred log estimate into dollars gives a larger range, 10.3 to 14.6 percent, or \$291 to \$413 per lease-year. Because these translations require choices about the dollar outcome and the evaluation point, we use the 40-to-20-share effect, 8.7 percent, as the main causal magnitude. We also report a simpler model calibration based on a dollar regression without controls or fixed effects. That calibration implies a smaller gap of 3.0 percent of lease income, or about \$85 per lease-year, which we treat as a lower benchmark.

The paper contributes to the study of bureaucracy by showing that the distribution of stakes among an agency's beneficiaries affects the output the agency secures for them. Existing work studies how Congress, the President, and agency objectives shape bureaucratic behavior \citep{Fiorina1977, KiewietMcCubbins1991, Weingast1984, Moe1985, WestCooper1989, Niskanen1971, Dixit2002, GailmardPatty2012}. Other work studies street-level discretion, intrinsic motivation, and biased or over-demanding clients \citep{Lipsky2010, BesleyGhatak2005, Prendergast2003, Prendergast2007, ForandUjhelyiTing2022}. In much of this work, client interactions are private, because clients monitor to obtain services or benefits for themselves. We study a different multiprincipal agency problem. A bureaucracy produces one output for a group of beneficiaries, and monitoring by any one beneficiary raises the return to all. The public-good problem among dispersed principals can therefore shape the output of a shared agent even within one administrative regime.

Field experiments show that community monitoring can improve public services. In Uganda, community monitoring increased health workers' effort and improved child health \citep{BjorkmanSvensson2009}. In Indonesian village road projects, government audits reduced missing expenditures, but grassroots monitoring did so only where free-rider problems and elite capture were limited \citep{Olken2007}. These experiments changed community monitoring by design and measured it directly. We instead use quasi-random variation in how the beneficiaries' stakes are distributed and measure its effect on a market outcome, the rent the agency secures. The question also parallels the literature on large shareholders, in which a shareholder with a large stake monitors management because it captures a large share of the gains \citep{ShleiferVishny1986}. In firms, investors choose their holdings, so ownership concentration is itself an outcome \citep{DemsetzLehn1985}. Among the co-owners of a tract, the timing of inheritance supplies variation in the concentration of stakes that the design treats as quasi-random.

The paper also contributes to work on American Indian land tenure and fragmented ownership. \citet{LeonardParker2021} study oil production on the Fort Berthold Reservation and show that fragmented ownership reduces the use of a spatially expansive natural resource. The closest related paper is \citet{DippelFryeLeonard2025}, who estimate the long-run cost of nontransferable trust title by comparing allotted-trust parcels with nearby fee-simple parcels and measuring development and cultivation with satellite data. We study a different margin inside the trust regime: how observed ownership fractionation on leased allotted trust tracts affects the lease income the BIA secures. Their paper estimates the cost of restrictions on transfer rights. We estimate the bureaucratic-output cost of fragmented beneficiary stakes, holding trust status fixed. Reservation institutions also shape development: \citet{Dippel2014} shows that reservations that combined autonomous bands under shared governance are about 30 percent poorer today. Our comparisons are within reservations, so such reservation-level institutions are held fixed.

The estimates apply to leased, multi-owner agricultural tracts on allotted trust land where the BIA acts as the lease administrator, and 85.7 percent of the leases in the preferred IV sample are in Montana, the Dakotas, and Oklahoma. Within the relevant population, the lease records are close to a census of the activity to which the mechanism applies. The broader implication is that beneficiary-side collective action can affect how much output a bureaucracy secures even among claims administered by the same agency.

\section{Theory} \label{Theory}

Consider a bureaucracy that produces output $R$ for a group of $n$ beneficiaries. The bureaucracy is the agent. The beneficiaries are principals who receive shares of its output and may monitor its effort. A beneficiary may contribute monitoring, denoted by $m_i$, and we refer to a beneficiary who chooses $m_i>0$ as a monitoring contributor. Output increases with bureaucratic effort $e$, and effort increases with aggregate monitoring $M$.
\footnote{This assumption follows \citet{Prendergast2003} and \citet{Lipsky2010}, who emphasize that bureaucratic effort responds to monitoring.} Aggregate monitoring is the sum of individual contributions, $M=\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i$. Because monitoring by one beneficiary can raise output for all, monitoring is a public good among the principals.

Beneficiary $i$ has utility $u_i(R(e(\sum_{k=1}^n m_k)), w_i-c_i m_i)$, where $w_i$ is the endowment and $c_i$ is the unit cost of monitoring. Because $R$ and $e$ increase with $M$, the first argument is the public good that the bureaucracy generates. The public good depends on aggregate monitoring, so each beneficiary's contribution raises it for all. The second argument is the private good left after monitoring costs.

We assume that the public and private goods are perfect substitutes. Public-goods theory treats perfect substitutability as a special case \citep{Varian1992, BatinaIhori2005}. In this setting, the assumption is natural because the public good is monetary or money-like output produced through costly monitoring. A beneficiary is willing to spend up to one dollar of private resources to obtain one dollar of bureaucracy-generated output. If a city fails to collect garbage, for example, a resident is willing to contact the agency or file complaints until the cost of doing so equals the cost of private collection. The same logic applies to other bureaucracy-provided services such as roads, education, policing, and, in our application, lease income.

We solve a one-shot, simultaneous-move contribution game under complete information. The model has two standard implications. With equal stakes and identical costs, the symmetric equilibrium pins down total monitoring and therefore the relationship between group size and output. With unequal stakes, the beneficiary with the highest marginal value of the public good contributes monitoring, up to ties, while the others free-ride \citep{Olson1965, BergstromBlumeVarian1986}. We now derive functional relationships that map these implications to beneficiary stakes. In the empirical setting, these stakes are ownership shares.

Let bureaucratic effort be $e(M)=M/(\gamma+M)$. This function is concave on $[0, \infty)$, ranges from zero to one, and makes $\gamma$ the parameter governing the responsiveness of effort to monitoring. A larger $\gamma$ means that monitoring translates into effort more slowly. We adopt this form because it is parsimonious, delivers closed-form equilibrium expressions, and has the boundary behavior the setting requires: zero effort at zero monitoring, and effort approaching one as monitoring grows without bound. Any increasing, strictly concave effort function with the same boundary conditions yields the same qualitative comparative statics. The specific form pins down the functional relationships we take to the data, including the $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ measure. Let $R_l$ denote the maximum attainable output, determined by factors outside bureaucratic effort. For a police department, for example, solved crimes are bounded by criminal activity. In the leasing setting below, attainable rent is bounded by the tract's agricultural value and the local lease market. Realized output is
\begin{equation} \label{(1)}
    R(M, R_l) = \frac{M}{\gamma + M}R_l.
\end{equation}
The derivations below characterize interior monitoring levels, which deliver the exact expressions used in the empirical mapping. If an unconstrained monitoring expression is negative, the nonnegativity constraint sets the relevant contribution to zero and the same comparative statics hold weakly.

Each beneficiary maximizes her share of bureaucratic output net of monitoring costs.\footnote{The public goods we consider are divisible, so beneficiaries can receive different shares of the output.} We use the quasilinear payoff $\pi_i=r_i-c_i m_i$, where $r_i$ is beneficiary $i$'s share of bureaucratic output, $m_i$ is her monitoring contribution, and $c_i$ is her unit cost of monitoring.

Consider equal shares and identical monitoring costs, and normalize the unit monitoring cost to 1. Before turning to private incentives, we define an efficiency benchmark. A planner who could set aggregate monitoring $M$ directly would choose it to maximize total beneficiary surplus, which is output net of monitoring cost,
\[
    \Pi = R - M = \frac{M}{\gamma + M} R_l - M,
\]
taking the bureaucratic production function, the unit monitoring cost, and the ceiling $R_l$ as given. We call the resulting level constrained efficient: efficient because it maximizes total surplus, and constrained because the only lever is monitoring, not the agency's technology or the ceiling $R_l$. The first-order condition $R_l\gamma/(\gamma + M)^2 = 1$ gives
\begin{equation} \label{(2)}
    M^* = \sqrt{R_l \gamma} - \gamma.
\end{equation}
The benchmark $M^*$ is the yardstick for the equilibrium derived below.

Equation \eqref{(2)} shows that the constrained efficient monitoring benchmark is independent of the number of beneficiaries. The corresponding output is 
\begin{equation} \label{(3)}
    R^*=R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}.
\end{equation}

When monitoring is provided privately, each beneficiary ignores the monitoring benefit she confers on the others, so private provision falls short of $M^*$ whenever the output is shared. With identical beneficiaries, beneficiary $i$ maximizes $\displaystyle \pi_i=\frac{R}{n}-m_i$, subject to $m_i \geq 0$. Since $M$ is the sum of all contributions, $M=m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j$. Substituting for $R$ and $M$ in beneficiary $i$'s objective function gives
\[
\pi_i=\frac{m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j}{\gamma + m_i+\sum_{j\neq i} m_j} \frac{R_l}{n}-m_i.
\]
The first-order condition is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \pi_i}{\partial m_i} = \frac{\gamma}{(\gamma + m_i +\sum_{j\neq i} m_j)^2} \frac{R_l}{n} - 1 = 0. \nonumber
\end{equation}
It implies
\begin{equation}
    m_i + \sum_{j\neq i} m_j = \frac{\sqrt{R_l\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma, \nonumber
\end{equation}
and therefore total monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(4)}
    M = \frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma.
\end{equation}

$M<M^*$ if $n>1$, so monitoring is underprovided relative to the constrained efficient benchmark when there are multiple beneficiaries of bureaucratic output. Monitoring decreases in $n$. When $n=1$, then $M=M^*$. Substituting $M$ from equation \eqref{(4)} into \eqref{(1)} gives realized bureaucratic output under equal shares.
\begin{equation}
    R=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma}{\gamma +\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-\gamma} R_l \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(5)}
    R= R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{n}.
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{(5)} predicts that bureaucracy-generated output decreases in the number of beneficiaries when beneficiaries have equal shares and identical costs. The gap between the constrained efficient output $R^*$ and realized output is
\begin{equation}
    R^*-R=(R_l - \sqrt{R_l \gamma})-(R_l-\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{n}) \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(6)}
    R^*-R=- \sqrt{R_l\gamma} + \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{n}.
\end{equation}

Now let beneficiaries hold unequal shares of bureaucratic output while retaining identical monitoring costs. The constrained efficient amount of monitoring remains the same as in the equal-share case. Privately, however, beneficiary $i$ maximizes $\pi_i=s_i R-m_i$, where $s_i$ is the beneficiary's output share. The first-order condition analogous to the one above gives the reaction function
\begin{equation} \label{(7)}
    m_i = \sqrt{s_i} \sqrt{R_l \gamma} -\gamma -\sum_{j\neq i}{m_j}.
\end{equation}

Let $\bar m_i=\sqrt{s_i}\sqrt{R_l\gamma}-\gamma$ denote the monitoring contribution beneficiary $i$ would choose if no other beneficiary contributed. Because $R$ is concave in $M$, beneficiary $i$'s payoff is concave in $m_i$, and because monitoring contributions cannot be negative, the best response is $m_i=\max\{\bar m_i-\sum_{j\neq i}m_j,0\}$. If one beneficiary has the unique largest share, that beneficiary has the highest $\bar m_i$ and contributes all monitoring in the simultaneous-move, complete-information game. All other beneficiaries free-ride. If several beneficiaries tie for the largest share, the allocation of monitoring among them is not unique, but aggregate monitoring and output are unchanged. In the empirical setting, beneficiaries are owners. We therefore denote the largest ownership share by $s_{max}$.

The total amount of monitoring provided is
\begin{equation} \label{(8)}
    M = \sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}}-\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the output generated by the bureaucracy is
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}}-\gamma}{\gamma + \sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_{max}} - \gamma}R_l \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(9)}
    R = R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent
The output gap is
\begin{equation}
    R^*-R = (R_l -\sqrt{R_l \gamma})-(R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right)) \nonumber
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{(10)}
    R^*-R =-\sqrt{R_l\gamma} + \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{max}}}\right).
\end{equation}
\noindent
The model therefore predicts that bureaucracy-generated output increases with the largest owner's share.

The model can also accommodate beneficiaries who differ in monitoring costs. Appendix~A (``The Organized-Owner Extension'') develops this case for an organized owner, meaning an owner whose organizational form or formal access to the agency lowers its unit cost of monitoring, and the individual owner with the largest ownership share. The equilibrium contributor is the owner with the larger cost-adjusted ownership share, $s_g$ for the organized owner, whose cost is normalized to one, or $s_I/c_I$ for the largest individual owner. An organized owner such as a tribe can therefore contribute all monitoring without holding the largest ownership share if its monitoring cost is sufficiently low. The extension implies that lower monitoring costs raise monitoring and output.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Hypothesis 1.} With equal shares and identical monitoring costs, bureaucratic output decreases in the number of beneficiaries (equation~\eqref{(5)}). Each beneficiary receives the share $1/n$ of the output, so its private return to monitoring falls as $n$ rises.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Hypothesis 2.} With unequal shares and identical monitoring costs, bureaucratic output increases with the largest beneficiary's share $s_{max}$ (equation~\eqref{(9)}). The beneficiary with the largest share supplies all monitoring, and its private return to monitoring rises with its share.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Hypothesis 3.} When monitoring costs differ, bureaucratic output increases with the largest cost-adjusted share, so it rises as the unit monitoring cost of the contributing owner falls (equations~\eqref{(14)} and~\eqref{(17)}). An organized owner such as a tribe can therefore raise output without holding the largest share.




\section{The BIA and American Indian Trust Land} \label{Background}

\subsection{Allotment, trust status, and fractionation} \label{History}

Allotted trust land matters for this paper because federal law separated beneficial ownership from the ordinary rights to sell, mortgage, or partition land. The General Allotment Act of 1887, commonly called the Dawes Act, authorized the federal government to survey reservation land and issue individual allotments to American Indians. The statutory schedule assigned 160 acres to each head of family, 80 acres to each single adult and orphan child, and 40 acres to other minors, with additional acreage where land was useful only for grazing. After allotments were made, land remaining within a reservation could be declared surplus and opened to non-Indian settlement. Allotment was an assimilation policy that sought to replace tribal landholding with individual farming and individual title. It also transferred large amounts of land out of Indian ownership. Indian landholdings fell from roughly 138 million acres before allotment to roughly 48 million acres by 1934 \citep{Carlson1981,Otis2014,McDonnell1991,DippelFryeLeonard2025}.\footnote{General Allotment Act of 1887, ch. 119, 24 Stat. 388, \S\S 1, 5. The Bureau of Indian Affairs describes over 90 million acres as passing out of Indian ownership between the Dawes Act and 1934 \citep{BIAIndianLandConsolidationHistory}.} The legal structure created by allotment continues to shape modern Indian land tenure \citep{Royster1995,Shoemaker2020}.

Most allotments did not convey unrestricted fee-simple title at the outset. Under section 5 of the Dawes Act, the United States held allotted land in trust for the allottee or the allottee's heirs. The allottee held beneficial ownership, including the right to use the land and receive income from it, but did not hold the full bundle of ordinary private property rights. Trust interests could not be sold, encumbered, mortgaged, or taxed without federal action. The Burke Act of 1906 added an early-exit mechanism. If the Secretary of the Interior judged an allottee competent and capable of managing his or her affairs, the Secretary could issue a fee-simple patent. After a fee patent, the land became taxable and could be sold or encumbered without the same federal restrictions.\footnote{Burke Act of 1906, ch. 2348, 34 Stat. 182.}

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended new allotments and extended the trust periods and restrictions on alienation for remaining trust allotments.\footnote{Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, ch. 576, 48 Stat. 984, codified as amended at 25 U.S.C. \S 5101 et seq. Section 5101 ended new allotment. Section 5102 extended existing trust periods and restrictions on alienation.} This did not ban all land transfers. It did mean that owners generally could not use the ordinary private-market routes by which co-owners consolidate tenancy in common, including unrestricted sale, mortgage, or partition under state property law.

These restrictions interacted with inheritance law. Before 1910, allottees generally could not pass trust allotments to heirs by will. Congress authorized wills in 1910, but a will covering trust property had no legal force unless the Secretary approved it.\footnote{Act of June 25, 1910, ch. 431, \S 2, 36 Stat. 856, codified as amended at 25 U.S.C. \S 373.} For much of the twentieth century, if an owner died without an approved will, the owner's interest descended under state or territorial rules incorporated by federal law. Modern probate is governed by federal and approved tribal rules under the Indian Land Consolidation Act and the American Indian Probate Reform Act. Across these regimes, heirs commonly received undivided shares as tenants in common. Each heir received a fractional claim on the whole tract rather than a demarcated physical piece of land. Because consolidation generally required federal approval or a probate-related mechanism, each probate tended to add owners and reduce the size of individual shares \citep{Shoemaker2003,Shoemaker2020,DippelFryeLeonard2025}.

We refer to the resulting dispersion of undivided ownership shares as ownership fractionation. These undivided interests determine both how lease income is divided and each owner's incentive to monitor the BIA. The Supreme Court's description of Tract 1305 illustrates the scale of the problem. The 40-acre tract produced \$1,080 in annual income, had 439 owners, and cost the BIA an estimated \$17,560 per year to administer. One-third of its owners received less than five cents a year in rent, and the smallest heir's share paid a cent every 177 years \citep{HodelIrving1987}. Tract 1305 is extreme but not unique: in our lease records, 24 tracts have at least as many owners. For most of these owners, the rent at stake is too small to repay much effort, although together their claims make up the whole tract's income.

Congress and the Department of the Interior have tried to reduce fractionation while keeping land in trust. These reforms show that transfers are possible, but consolidation generally proceeds through federally supervised mechanisms rather than ordinary private partition or unrestricted sale. The April 2010 ownership snapshot predates congressional approval of the Cobell settlement in December 2010 and the Department of the Interior's establishment of the Land Buy-Back Program in December 2012. The estimates therefore describe fractionation before that program began purchasing fractional interests.\footnote{The Indian Land Consolidation Act of 1983 initially provided that very small interests escheated, that is, passed to the tribe at the owner's death instead of passing by inheritance or by will. The Supreme Court invalidated that rule in \citet{HodelIrving1987}. Later reforms relied on probate rules, consolidation agreements, and voluntary acquisition. The American Indian Probate Reform Act of 2004 created federal probate rules for most individually owned trust and restricted land and authorized purchase options at probate for eligible heirs, co-owners, and tribes. Congress approved the Cobell settlement through the Claims Resolution Act of 2010. The settlement provided a \$1.9 billion fund for voluntary purchases of fractional interests that were restored to tribal trust ownership \citep{AIPRA2004,ClaimsResolutionAct2010,DOILandBuyBackProgram2023}.}

\subsection{BIA agricultural leasing} \label{Leasing}

The BIA administers the trust land on which the leases in our data are signed. It maintains land-title and probate records, approves conveyances, and manages trust-land leases. The leases in our sample were signed under the agricultural-leasing regulations at 25 C.F.R. Part 162, Subpart B, as issued in 2001.\footnote{66 Fed. Reg. 7068 (Jan. 22, 2001), effective March 23, 2001 \citep{DOI2001LeasingRule}. Section numbers in this paper refer to that rule \citep{CFR2008Part162}. The 2012 revision of Part 162 states that it does not affect Subpart B, Agricultural Leases \citep[p.~72440]{BIA2012}.} Indian landowners may grant agricultural leases of their own interests, including undivided interests in fractionated tracts, subject to BIA approval. The owners of a majority of the trust interests in a tract may grant a lease that binds all Indian owners, provided that the owners who do not consent receive a fair annual rental. The BIA may itself grant a lease for owners it represents, such as orphaned minors, undetermined heirs, owners whose whereabouts are unknown, and owners who have given it a power of attorney. It may also grant a lease for all owners of a fractionated tract when, after written notice, the owners have not agreed on a lease within three months and no Indian owner is using the land.\footnote{See 25 U.S.C. \S\S 380, 3715(c)(2) and 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.107, 162.207(c), 162.209. The regulations use \emph{grant} to describe the act of entering into the lease. The American Indian Agricultural Resource Management Act of 1993 relaxed earlier unanimity requirements \citep{AmericanIndianAgriculturalResourceManagementAct1993}. Under the earlier regulations, a lease negotiated by the owners of a majority interest bound only the interests that the Secretary represented (25 C.F.R. \S 162.6 (2000)). The sliding consent schedule of 25 U.S.C. \S 2218 does not modify the 1993 Act (\S 2218(f)), so agricultural leases require a simple majority at every owner count.}

BIA lease services include contacting owners, advertising or assisting negotiation, determining fair annual rental, approving lease terms, recording the lease, collecting rent when payment runs through the agency, and enforcing lease obligations. Fair annual rental is the rent a tract would most probably command in an open and competitive market. The BIA determines it before granting or approving a lease, by competitive bidding, appraisal, or another appropriate valuation method, and it must obtain fair annual rental when it grants a lease on the owners' behalf.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.101, 162.107, 162.211, 162.222. Rent may fall below fair annual rental only when the tenant is a co-owner, an immediate family member, or an entity of the owners, or when the land has been advertised and the tenant is the highest responsible bidder (25 U.S.C. \S 3715(a)(2); 25 C.F.R. \S 162.222(c)--(d)).} On fractionated tracts, the agency realty office is central to setting lease terms. Before a lease expires, the agency obtains a market survey, rent study, or appraisal of the tract. It then notifies each owner that the owners have 90 days to negotiate a new lease, usually with a prospective lessee who initiates the negotiation. If no acceptable lease is negotiated, the agency generally advertises the tract for sealed bids against a minimum based on its valuation and grants the lease on behalf of all owners \citep[pp.~6--9, 15--17]{BIA2006Ch2}.\footnote{\citet[pp.~27--29]{GAO1999} describes the same sequence. Interior Inspector General audits describe sealed-bid lease sales at the Fort Peck and Rosebud agencies in the mid-1990s \citep{DOIOIG1998FortPeck, DOIOIG1998Rosebud}. At the Fort Berthold Agency, 110 of the 118 agricultural leases that expired in 1996 were advertised in the agency's February 1997 lease sale, according to agency records cited in the BIA's response to the Inspector General. Before bidding closed, landowners withdrew 23 parcels to negotiate leases themselves \citep{DOIOIG1998FortBerthold}. In \textit{Collins v. Acting Billings Area Director}, 30 IBIA 165 (1997), the Interior Board of Indian Appeals reviewed a Wind River lease that the Superintendent negotiated for an allotment with at least 96 owners, a number that made ``negotiations by the landowners very difficult, if not impossible.''} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:leasing_steps} lists the steps, and Exhibits A and B in the appendix reproduce the BIA's sample notice to owners and its sample sale notice.

The highest bid normally wins, but the superintendent may reject any or all bids, and a single bid below the BIA's valuation may be raised orally to an acceptable amount at the sale \citep[p.~16 and Attachment~14A]{BIA2006Ch2}. Negotiated rents and bids are both compared with that valuation. Agency officials interviewed for a 1999 General Accounting Office report said that they treated the appraised rent as the minimum acceptable rent, because they feared that approving less would lead Indian landowners to file appeals or lawsuits \citep[p.~13]{GAO1999}. No statute required an appraisal, and the 1993 Act permits a lease below the appraisal after advertising (25 U.S.C. \S 3715(a)(2)). The prospect of owner challenges is one way in which owners monitor the agency's leasing decisions. Of the 1,013 decisions in the chronological indexes of the Interior Board of Indian Appeals for 1999 to 2007, 19 concern appeals by Indian landowners in disputes with the BIA over agricultural or grazing leases, permits, trespass, or rent. In 6 of them, the appellant owned at least 91 percent of an allotment or was one of four or five co-owners. In 5, co-owners appealed together, and in 2 the appellant held a small interest. The other 6 decisions do not describe the appellant's interest.\footnote{Our count, from the Board's chronological indexes and the text of each decision. The appellant owned at least 91 percent of an allotment in \textit{Grant v. Acting Billings Area Director}, 33 IBIA 156 (1999); \textit{Smith v. Acting Anadarko Area Director}, 34 IBIA 283 (2000); and \textit{Byrd v. Acting Great Plains Regional Director}, 45 IBIA 130 (2007). The appellant was one of four or five co-owners in \textit{Dailey v. Billings Area Director}, 34 IBIA 128 (1999); \textit{Jones v. Billings Area Director}, 36 IBIA 104 (2001); and \textit{Palmer v. Acting Southern Plains Regional Director}, 43 IBIA 268 (2006). Co-owners appealed together in \textit{Tendoy v. Portland Area Director}, 33 IBIA 303 (1999); \textit{Papse v. Acting Portland Area Director}, 33 IBIA 175 (1999); \textit{Denny v. Northwest Regional Director}, 36 IBIA 220 (2001); \textit{27 Individual Landowners on the Standing Rock Reservation v. Great Plains Regional Director}, 40 IBIA 239 (2005); and \textit{Wadsworth v. Northwest Regional Director}, 41 IBIA 172 (2005). Two decisions state small interests, one of 32 owners in \textit{Hidalgo v. Acting Portland Area Director}, 35 IBIA 141 (2000), and 2.9 percent in \textit{Mandan v. Acting Great Plains Regional Director}, 40 IBIA 206 (2005).}

A lease runs for a definite term. The term may not exceed 10 years, or 25 years when the lease requires a substantial investment, and it may not exceed 2 years when all interests belong to a decedent whose heirs have not been determined. A lease may not give the tenant an option to renew, and it may not be renewed or extended by holdover. A tenant who stays after the term ends is treated as a trespasser unless the BIA believes that the tenant is negotiating a new lease with the owners.\footnote{25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.229, 162.256; 66 Fed. Reg. at 7084 \citep{DOI2001LeasingRule}. The earlier regulations also barred preference rights to future leases (25 C.F.R. \S 162.5 (2000)). Unless a tribal leasing policy lets Indian bidders match the highest responsible bid, advertisements may not offer any bidder a preference, including the sitting tenant (25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.203(b)(1), 162.212(b)).} Each expiring lease therefore returns to the process described above, and a sitting tenant who wants to continue must obtain a new lease, by negotiating with the owners or by bidding at the sale. Rent is fixed for the term, and a lease longer than 5 years must provide for rent adjustments (25 C.F.R. \S 162.223).

In the preferred IV sample, 84.6 percent of leases have a 5-year term. The lease records show rent fixed within a lease and reset when a tract is re-let. In the BIA's audited payment ledger for fiscal year 2008, 85.0 percent of the 6,070 agricultural leases in force throughout the year with one annual invoice show the same annual rent, within 1 percent, in the 2010 record. Of the 915 leases that we can link to an expired predecessor, almost all in the Great Plains region, 4.7 percent kept the predecessor's rent (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:leasing_facts}).

Leases specify how rent is paid. Under agency pay, the lessee pays the BIA, and the agency distributes each owner's share through the trust-account system. Under direct pay, the lease specifies that the lessee pays the landowners directly, subject to BIA approval and documentation requirements.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. \S 162.226.} Leases awarded at a BIA lease sale are paid to the agency, and the BIA's sample sale notice states that every lease approved under it will be agency pay \citep[Attachment~14A]{BIA2006Ch2}. For leases approved from October 2007, a BIA policy also required rent on owner-negotiated leases to be paid to the agency unless every owner agreed to direct payment \citep{BIA2007NPM}. The policy barred direct payment whenever an interest was held in an estate or in a supervised, encumbered, or whereabouts-unknown account. Direct payment therefore marks leases that owners arranged themselves. Agency payment marks BIA collection, which on fractionated tracts typically, though not exclusively, accompanies leasing run by the BIA. The payment route also changes enforcement. When rent is paid to the BIA, the agency must issue a notice of violation within five business days after a missed due date. Under direct pay, it acts only after the owners notify it of the nonpayment (25 C.F.R. \S 162.248(a)). We use direct payment later as a proxy for owner involvement.

Some allotted tracts include tribal undivided ownership interests. Tribal interests can arise through consolidation, probate, or other federally approved conveyances that move fractional interests into tribal ownership. A tribal co-owner receives a share of the lease income like any other co-owner. Tribal governments also have regular government-to-government channels with the BIA, which may lower the cost of monitoring the agency. This is why tribal ownership is informative about the model's implication that lower monitoring costs raise monitoring and output. A tribe can also shape the leasing of allotted land through a general leasing policy adopted by tribal resolution, for example one that lets Indian bidders match the highest responsible bid. The owners of at least half of the trust interests in a tract may exempt it from such a policy, and tribal rental rates, like tribal laws that supersede the leasing regulations, apply only to tribal land.\footnote{25 U.S.C. \S 3715(b)--(c); 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.109(b), 162.203--162.205, 162.222(b).}

Some tracts also include fee-simple undivided interests. A fee-simple interest can arise when a trust or restricted interest leaves federal supervision, including through a fee patent or removal of restrictions. The interest can remain an undivided share of the same tract. The BIA administers the lease for the trust and restricted interests, and the aggregate trust or restricted share must be identified when the tract also contains fee-simple interests. This distinction matters because the lease-income outcome records rent on the trust and restricted interests that the BIA administers, not rent on the unrestricted fee-simple share.\footnote{See 25 C.F.R. Part 162, Subpart B, and 25 C.F.R. \S 152.6. Section 152.6 allows the Secretary to issue a patent in fee for trust land or an interest in trust land acquired by inheritance or devise by a non-Indian or by a person of Indian descent to whom the United States owes no trust responsibility.}

A minimum rent does not make the rent mechanical. The BIA's valuation sets a floor for bids and a reference point for negotiated rents, but the rent on a lease is the winning bid or the negotiated amount. Agency staff control the steps between the floor and that rent. Staff decide whether to commission a new valuation of the tract or to rely on an earlier market survey, so the floor itself may be current or stale. They decide when and how widely to advertise, which affects how many bidders compete. In 1998, bids arrived on 69 percent of the tracts that the Fort Peck agency advertised but on 7 percent of those that the Yakama agency advertised \citep[p.~29]{GAO1999}. They decide whether to reject low bids and advertise again, to raise a single low bid to the floor at the sale, or to accept a bid below the valuation, which the law permits after advertising. They also decide how quickly to approve and enforce the lease.

Owners can act at each of these steps. They can negotiate during the notice period, withdraw a tract from a sale to negotiate a lease themselves, ask the agency to advertise, report lease violations, and appeal an approval to which they did not consent. The BIA must begin to investigate a reported violation within five business days.\footnote{25 U.S.C. \S 3715(a)(2); 25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.209(b), 162.212(a), 162.222(d), 162.250(b), and Part 2; \citet[pp.~15--20]{BIA2006Ch2}.}

A floor applied regardless of demand would leave land unleased rather than lower the rent on leased land. An Interior working group quoted by the General Accounting Office found in 1987 that strict application of appraised rents had left land unleased \citep{GAO1999}. Rents in our data do not follow a schedule. Within a reservation and signing year, two leases in the preferred IV sample charge the identical rent per trust acre, to the cent, in 0.66 percent of pairs. The rent changes of re-let tracts also vary from tract to tract (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:leasing_facts}). A binding floor alone therefore cannot produce the negative relation between fractionation and rent among leased tracts. The relation must arise in what bidders offer and what the agency accepts, which is where owners' attention can change the agency's effort.

These institutional facts map directly to the model. A leased tract produces a single output, the annual lease income on its trust portion. Owners receive that income in proportion to their undivided ownership interests. If one owner contacts the BIA, supplies information about land use, presses for a higher rent when the tract is re-let, or monitors compliance, all co-owners benefit through the same lease income. Monitoring is therefore a public good among the co-owners of a tract. Inheritance-generated fractionation changes the distribution of claims among beneficiaries while leaving the BIA as the common lease administrator for many signed leases. Fractionation can also reduce land use for a different reason. Assembling owner consent is harder when a tract has more owners, and that consent-cost channel matters most for whether a lease is signed, the extensive margin. Because the owners of a majority of the trust interests can grant a lease that binds the others, consent costs fall discretely when a single owner holds such a majority. We use this threshold to test the consent-cost channel. The main estimates speak to the intensive margin of lease income per trust acre, not to whether land is leased at all.

\section{Data} \label{Data}

Our data come from the Trust Asset and Accounting Management System, or TAAMS, the Department of the Interior's system of record for title and land-resource management of Indian trust and restricted land \citep{DOITAAMSSORN2014}.\footnote{We received the anonymized administrative extract from a senior officer at the BIA. The version used for analysis does not contain owner names or direct personal identifiers.} TAAMS records land ownership, contracts and leases, beneficial owners, and trust-asset management information. The main analytic extract is an April 2010 snapshot of all trust and restricted tracts, the ownership interests recorded for them at that date, and the leases and other encumbrances active in 2010. We focus on agricultural leases signed between 2006 and 2010. In the constructed agricultural sample, 96.4 percent of leases have terms of five years or less. The restriction keeps the lease date close to the ownership snapshot for most leases. A separate September 28, 2009 ownership snapshot provides a short-run validation of the ownership measures (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ownership_snapshot_validation}). Deaths and probate orders between lease signing and April 2010 can still make the snapshot ownership structure more fractionated than the ownership structure in force when a lease was signed.

The unit of observation is an agricultural lease on a single tract of allotted trust land. We keep leases that cover exactly one tract, so each observation has one ownership structure and one lease outcome.\footnote{Some leases cover several tracts with different ownership structures. Including them would require aggregating ownership across tracts and assigning one contract payment to tracts with different ownership claims. Restricting to one-tract leases avoids those aggregation choices.} For each lease we observe the recorded lease-income field, lease-signing year, lease term, anonymized owner identifiers and undivided ownership interests, reservation, tract acreage, payment arrangement, tribal ownership, fee-simple share, and whether the tract's trust estate holds the surface only or the surface and the minerals. The extract's parcel identifiers encode each tract's location in the Public Land Survey System, which we use to attach terrain and soil measures to each tract.\footnote{The raw parcel identifier encodes Public Land Survey System geography below the reservation. A tract corresponds to one or more of these parcels, so we recover section-, quarter-section-, or aliquot-level parcel locations from the Bureau of Land Management's national PLSS data \citep{BLMNationalPLSS}, assign map units from the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO) \citep{SoilSurveyStaffSSURGO} to sampled points within each parcel, and aggregate parcel values to the tract using acreage weights. In the preferred IV sample, 6,230 of 6,337 tracts can be geolocated. The representative parcel locations used in the matching resolve 3,472 tracts at the section level, 1,637 at the quarter-section level, and 1,121 at the aliquot level. The common land-characteristic comparison retains 6,039 leases across 42 reservations after complete-case and fixed-effect processing. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_characteristics_sensitivity} reports the matching details and the resulting sensitivity estimates.}

We construct ownership measures from recorded undivided ownership interests that define the owners of the tract for lease administration. We exclude beneficial-only interests because they do not represent current undivided ownership interests used to administer the lease.\footnote{The excluded TAAMS beneficial-only interest-type codes are C, D, H, I, L, M, Q, R, S, and W.} From the remaining interests, we compute the number of owners, the largest owner's share, the ownership Herfindahl index, the shares held by the largest two, five, and ten owners, an indicator for whether a tribe is the largest owner, and the fee-simple share of the tract.

TAAMS separates lease payments made to the BIA for distribution from lease payments made directly to landowners. We measure direct payment in two ways. The directly paid share is the fraction of a lease's income paid directly to landowners. The direct-payment indicator equals one when any part of the income is paid directly. We call these leases direct-payment leases and the rest agency-pay leases.

Lease income is observed only for leased tracts, so the estimation sample contains leased tracts only and the estimates are conditional on lease formation.

We then impose two restrictions that align the analysis with the individual-allotment channel in the model. First, we drop wholly tribal tracts. A wholly tribal tract has one owner and is leased through the tribe's government-to-government relationship with the BIA rather than through the individual-owner channel described by the model. We retain tracts in which a tribe holds a partial interest alongside individual owners and use that variation in the descriptive analyses of monitoring costs. We also drop the few leases involving a government or administrative account rather than an individual or tribal owner.

Second, we restrict the analysis to agricultural leases. For agricultural leases, annual lease income per trust acre is a natural measure of the output the BIA secures because lease payments reflect the surface use of land in local agricultural rental markets.\footnote{Among income-generating nonagricultural lease types in the extract, oil and gas leases are next most numerous. They are not comparable on a per-acre lease-income basis because payments depend on reserves, extraction costs, and operator-specific bargaining, not only on surface acreage.} Agricultural leasing falls under 25 C.F.R. Part 162. Grazing permits, governed by Part 166, are outside the sample. A grazing permit covers a range unit, which may combine many tracts, at a rate per animal-unit month that the BIA sets for individually owned land (25 C.F.R. \S\S 166.400--166.408). Permit income therefore does not measure what the agency secures for a single tract.

The outcome is the natural logarithm of annual lease income per trust acre. We interpret the recorded income field as the annual rental amount paid on the trust or restricted interests that the BIA administers.\footnote{The available extract specification does not define the field's periodicity. The scale of recorded lease income relative to USDA county agricultural rents and the predominance of one fiscal-year payment per agricultural lease document in the audited FY2008 BIA payment ledger support this interpretation.} When a tract also contains fee-simple undivided interests, TAAMS does not record lease payments for the fee-simple portion. We therefore divide recorded income by trust acreage, defined as total tract acreage multiplied by one minus the fee-simple share, so that the numerator and denominator describe the same BIA-administered portion of the tract. The baseline sample drops observations outside the first and ninety-ninth percentiles of income per trust acre. Robustness checks below retain the full positive-income sample and use alternative trimming thresholds.

The resulting constructed sample contains 9,409 leases across 52 reservations. Table~\ref{tab:table1} describes this sample and the 8,662 leases on tracts with more than one owner, in the 45 reservations with more than one such lease. The preferred IV sample keeps multi-owner, agency-pay leases with qualifying probate orders on or after 1938 and excludes the Crow Reservation. It contains 6,337 leases on 44 reservations. Every regression uses this sample, with three exceptions. The analysis of direct payment adds back the 631 direct-payment leases, for 6,968 leases, because the IV sample contains agency-pay leases only. The IV estimates that include Crow use 7,188 leases. The calibration uses the 8,387 leases of the constructed sample outside Crow, because it measures a dollar loss for agricultural trust leasing as a whole.

The preferred IV sample pools two kinds of tracts that differ in their trust estate. The trust estate of the 2,211 S tracts holds the surface only, and that of the 4,126 B tracts holds the surface and the minerals. The TAAMS resource code records this trust estate, not the use that a lease conveys. Oil and gas leases on B tracts are separate encumbrances, so their payments never enter the agricultural lease income we study. Within reservation and signing year, income per trust acre on B tracts does not differ detectably from income on S tracts. The difference is $-0.051$ log points, with a standard error of 0.044 and a wild-bootstrap $p$-value of 0.36 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:trust_estate}). The IV regressions, and every regression with lease controls, include an indicator for B tracts.\footnote{We do not restrict the sample to S tracts, because the earliest qualifying probate order predicts whether a tract is an S tract ($p<0.001$, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:trust_estate}). A sample of S tracts alone would therefore be selected on the instrument.}

\section{Empirical Strategy} \label{Econometrics}

Empirically, we use the lease data in two ways. The first is a descriptive least-squares exercise that tests whether lease income per trust acre covaries with ownership fractionation in the direction that Hypotheses 1 and 2 predict, after conditioning on reservation and lease-signing year. The second is the probate-timing instrumental-variables design that provides the paper's causal estimate. It uses the timing of inheritance events and estate processing to predict ownership fractionation within reservation and lease-signing year. The design does not require probate timing to be random in the raw data. A causal interpretation requires residual probate-order timing, after controls and fixed effects, to be conditionally unrelated to unobserved determinants of lease income and to affect lease income only through ownership fractionation.

The descriptive least-squares baseline is
\begin{equation} \label{(20)}
   y_{ijt} = \beta\, F_{ij} + X_{ijt}'\gamma + \mu_j + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ijt},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $y_{ijt}$ is log annual lease income per trust acre on tract $i$ on reservation $j$ for a lease signed in year $t$, $F_{ij}$ is a fractionation measure, $X_{ijt}$ is a vector of lease characteristics, and $\mu_j$ and $\delta_t$ are reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects. Reservation fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences across reservations in land quality, regulation, agency practice, and local rental markets. Lease-signing year fixed effects absorb common agricultural-rent shocks and production conditions in the year a lease is signed. In both the least-squares and the IV regressions, $X_{ijt}$ contains the lease term, the fee-simple share, log tract acreage, and an indicator for B tracts, whose trust estate also holds the minerals. We cluster standard errors by reservation, the level at which local agency practice, rental-market shocks, and residual correlation in the instrument are likely to operate.

The model motivates both the form of $F_{ij}$ and the sign of $\beta$. The primary measure is the inverse square root of the largest owner's share, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, which equation~\eqref{(9)} implies. Hypothesis 2 predicts $\beta<0$, because a larger top share lowers $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ and raises income. We also report the largest share entered linearly and as its inverse, which equals the number of owners when shares are equal. The square root of the owner count is the form that equation~\eqref{(5)} implies, and we also use its logarithm. The remaining measures are the combined share of the largest two, five, or ten owners and the ownership Herfindahl index. Under Hypotheses 1 and 2, less fractionation raises lease income per trust acre whichever measure captures it.

Equation~\eqref{(20)} is descriptive. Ownership structure is the product of more than a century of inheritance. Tracts that became heavily fractionated may differ in unobserved land productivity, location within the reservation, original allottee characteristics, or family history. The resulting bias in least squares can run either way. If poor land left families poorer and with fewer surviving heirs, less productive tracts would be less fractionated, and least squares would understate the income loss from fractionation. If instead families on poor land moved away and had large families, or valuable land was consolidated, less productive tracts would be more fractionated, and least squares would overstate the loss. The instrumental-variables design addresses this bias with residual variation in probate timing. Its validity rests on the conditions below and on their tests, not on the direction of the least-squares bias.

The IV design uses probate timing. Fractionation accumulates when an owner dies and a probate order divides the owner's undivided ownership interest among heirs. We instrument $F_{ij}$ with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders on the tract, denoted $Z_{ij}=(\underline{P}_{ij},\overline{P}_{ij})$. A probate order qualifies if it was approved no later than the lease effective date, the consistently recorded date tied to the beginning of the lease term.\footnote{TAAMS does not record a separate signing date for each lease, so the lease-signing year is the year of the effective date.} The first stage is
\begin{equation} \label{(21)}
   F_{ij} = Z_{ij}'\pi + X_{ijt}'\rho + \mu_j + \delta_t + \nu_{ijt}.
\end{equation}
\noindent Estimating equation~\eqref{(20)} by two-stage least squares, with $F_{ij}$ instrumented by $Z_{ij}$, has a causal interpretation under four conditions. Relevance requires probate timing to predict ownership fractionation. Conditional independence requires residual probate-order timing, after reservation fixed effects, lease-signing year fixed effects, and controls, to be uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of lease income. The no-direct-effect condition requires probate timing to affect lease income only through ownership fractionation. Monotonicity for the local interpretation requires changes in each probate-timing instrument to shift fractionation in its first-stage direction for otherwise comparable tracts, rather than reversing direction for a subset of tracts. Figure~\ref{fig:iv_dag} summarizes the intended path and the main threats to these conditions. We report the first stage with the IV estimates, together with diagnostics that probe specific threats to conditional independence and to the no-direct-effect condition.

Several channels could violate these conditions. Probate-order approval dates combine inheritance events and administrative processing. Land endowments and prior fractionation may shape both probate histories and lease income. Local administrative capacity may affect both probate processing and lease administration. Owners' longevity is a further channel. If fertile land made the allottee and later owners wealthier and longer-lived, the first probate order on a fertile tract would come later and its subsequent owners would die less often. Fertile tracts would then be less fractionated and also command higher rents, which would bias the IV estimate away from zero. Because each co-owner receives only a share of a tract's rental income, the effect of one tract's income on an owner's resources is attenuated by that share. Family wealth from land as a whole can still affect longevity. We test this path in two ways. We relate the death rates of current owners to measured soil quality, and we simulate the bias that the estimated relation implies.

With heterogeneous effects and a continuous ownership measure, two-stage least squares estimates a weighted average of causal responses for the margins of fractionation shifted by the probate-timing instruments \citep{MogstadTorgovitsky2018}. We therefore interpret the coefficient and the 40-to-20 translation as local to this probate-timing variation, not as global average effects for all allotted trust land. Policy discussions about consolidating fractional interests within trust should read the estimate with the same local qualification.

The IV identifies the total effect of ownership fractionation on lease income under the maintained assumptions. The monitoring model organizes the primary interpretation. Beyond monitoring, the total effect includes any effect of fractionation through accumulated investment in the land. It also includes any tendency of the BIA to devote less effort to tracts that are costlier to administer because they have more owners. Our design cannot separate either channel from monitoring. We study three secondary margins descriptively. Tribal co-ownership can lower the cost of contacting the BIA, which Hypothesis 3 links to higher output, and direct payment can proxy for greater owner involvement. Fee-simple share captures a different ownership mix and bargaining environment. We report these margins as descriptive associations after the IV estimates.

The preferred IV sample follows the institutional channel in the model. It contains agency-pay leases of fractionated tracts. For these leases, the BIA values the land, approves the rent against that valuation or grants the lease itself after advertising it, collects the rent, and enforces the lease on behalf of multiple owners. This definition excludes the Crow Reservation. During the sample period, 25 C.F.R. \S 162.500 allowed Crow Indians classified as competent to lease their trust lands and those of their minor children for farming or grazing without Secretarial approval, subject to statutory limits \citep{CFR2008Part162}. The 2012 revision of Part 162 moved this provision to \S 162.600 \citep{BIA2012}. Separate regulations govern the competency classification of Crow Indians \citep{ECFR25CFR153}. The extract contains a Crow-competent encumbrance code, but the available documentation leaves open whether that code identifies every competent agricultural lease, so the preferred sample excludes the reservation as a whole. It contains 6,337 leases on 44 reservations. The main IV exhibits also report Crow-included estimates for comparison, and Appendix~A (``Crow Competency-Leasing Regime and the IV Sample'') gives the regulatory details.

Inference accounts for the small number of reservation clusters. With 44 clusters in the preferred sample, conventional cluster-robust standard errors can over-reject. We therefore report wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values as the primary $p$-values \citep{CameronGelbachMiller2008}. Because two-stage least squares can also be sensitive to weak instruments, we report weak-instrument-robust Anderson--Rubin confidence sets computed with the same bootstrap \citep{AndersonRubin1949}. We also report conditional likelihood ratio confidence sets as a weak-instrument-robust complement. We measure first-stage strength with the effective $F$-statistic of \citet{MontielOleaPflueger2013}. With two instruments for one endogenous regressor, we report a test of the overidentifying restriction.

\section{Results} \label{Results}
\subsection{Descriptive ownership patterns}

We begin with the least-squares estimates, which are descriptive. They test whether lease income per trust acre is higher on tracts with more concentrated ownership after conditioning on reservation, lease-signing year, and the lease controls. They use the preferred IV sample of 6,337 leases on 44 reservations, so the descriptive and causal estimates rest on the same leases.

Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact} holds the sample and controls fixed and varies only the ownership measure. Column 1 uses the model-implied measure, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, which rises as the largest owner's share falls. The coefficient is $-0.0305$, with a standard error of 0.0135 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.051. Moving from a 40 percent largest-owner share to a 20 percent largest-owner share raises $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.655, so the estimate implies 0.020 log points, or 2.0 percent, lower lease income per trust acre.

Columns 2, 4, and 5 enter ownership concentration directly. A 10-percentage-point increase in the largest owner's share is associated with 0.011 log points higher income per trust acre in column 2, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.031. Moving the ownership Herfindahl index from zero to one is associated with 0.126 log points higher income in column 4, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.048. The combined share of the two largest owners gives the same sign in column 5, where the coefficient is 0.101 and the wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is 0.014. These columns measure concentration rather than fractionation, so Hypothesis 2 implies positive coefficients.

Owner count points in the same direction. Column 3 scales the count by 1,000 owners. The coefficient is $-0.583$, which implies that 100 additional owners are associated with 0.058 log points lower income, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.014. Hypothesis 1 predicts this pattern for equal shares, where the number of owners is the inverse of the largest share. Two tracts with the same number of owners can still differ in the largest share, and with unequal shares Hypothesis 2 makes that share the relevant measure.

Figures~\ref{fig1} and~\ref{fig2} show the same relations in binned form, after removing the fixed effects and controls of Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact}. Residualized lease income rises with the largest owner's share, and a binscatter test does not reject a linear relationship ($p=0.24$). Among tracts with no tribal ownership, it declines with the number of owners, and the test again does not reject linearity ($p=0.32$).

These associations are descriptive. Omitted land, allottee, and family characteristics can bias least squares in either direction, so we turn to the probate-timing instruments.

\subsection{Probate-timing IV estimates} \label{Identification}

Fractionation is the cumulative record of inheritance. Allotment initially assigned a tract to an individual owner. As that owner and later heirs died, probate orders divided undivided ownership interests among the next generation, and each order could add co-owners \citep{Shoemaker2014}. The 2010 ownership structure therefore reflects both how long a tract has been exposed to inheritance and how recently its interests continued to divide. We summarize that history with two instruments: the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date.

The instruments are relevant because earlier and more extensive inheritance histories produce more fractionated ownership. The identifying variation has two components: when owners died and when the agency processed the resulting estates. The evidence in this section has four roles. The first-stage and reduced-form results document the empirical links from probate timing to fractionation and lease income. The main IV table reports the estimate under the maintained IV assumptions. The timing, finer-geography, first-probate-decade, land-characteristic, and record-completeness exercises probe specific threats to those assumptions, and sensitivity bounds show how large a violation would have to be to overturn the estimate. Alternative ownership measures and leave-one-reservation-out checks assess functional-form robustness and influence.

Table~\ref{tab:iv_firststage} shows that the first stage is strong in the preferred Crow-excluded sample. Regressing the main fractionation measure on the two probate-timing instruments yields an effective $F$-statistic of 93.4 and a first-stage partial $R^2$ of 0.082. The signs have the expected interpretation. A later earliest qualifying probate order predicts less fractionation, with a coefficient of $-0.0052$ and a standard error of 0.0007. A later latest qualifying probate order predicts more fractionation, with a coefficient of 0.0154 and a standard error of 0.0014. The reduced-form coefficients move lease income in the corresponding direction. A later earliest order predicts higher lease income, with a coefficient of 0.0012 and a standard error of 0.0006. A later latest order predicts lower lease income, with a coefficient of $-0.0016$ and a standard error of 0.0009.

Figure~\ref{fig:iv_fs_rf} shows the same first-stage and reduced-form relationships using a standardized probate-timing index. Panel A plots the first stage. A one-standard-deviation increase in the index raises residualized $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.172, with a standard error of 0.016. Panel B plots the reduced form. The same index lowers residualized log lease income per trust acre by 0.0239, with a standard error of 0.0091. The ratio of these two slopes is the two-stage least-squares coefficient for the index specification. Figure~\ref{fig:iv_fs_rf} therefore shows why the IV estimate is negative.

The main IV estimate shows that fractionation lowers lease income per trust acre, as Hypothesis 2 predicts. In Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main}, the preferred row instruments the model-implied ownership measure, $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders. The two-stage least-squares coefficient is $-0.139$, with a standard error of 0.050 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.019. The Anderson--Rubin 95 percent confidence interval runs from $-0.242$ to $-0.020$, and the conditional likelihood ratio 95 percent confidence set, reported in the notes to Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main}, runs from $-0.266$ to $-0.022$. Moving from a largest-owner share of 40 percent to 20 percent raises $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ by 0.655. The estimate therefore implies a decline of 0.091 log points, or 8.7 percent, in lease income per trust acre. Conditional on four measures of soil productivity and condition, the estimate is $-0.094$, with a standard error of 0.043 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.046, on the 6,039 leases on 42 reservations with complete land data (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_characteristics_sensitivity}). That estimate implies a decline of 6.0 percent, and its Anderson--Rubin interval runs from $-0.157$ to $-0.029$.

The same-sample least-squares estimate in Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main}, which is also column 1 of Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact}, is much smaller. The least-squares coefficient implies a 40-to-20 decline of 2.0 percent, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.051. Sorting on observed land quality does not explain the gap (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_quality_ols}). More fractionated tracts differ from less fractionated ones by at most 0.04 standard deviations of any land measure per standard deviation of fractionation, and the differences run in both directions. Adding the soil measures moves the least-squares coefficient toward zero, from $-0.0270$ to $-0.0182$, and moves the IV coefficient by a similar share, so the ratio of the IV to the least-squares estimate changes only from 5.4 to 5.2. Two other sources of the gap are likely. Measurement error in recorded ownership shares would bias least squares toward zero. On the leases with complete soil data, owners who had died with their estates still pending made up 4.24 percent of all owners in September 2009. The IV estimate is also local to tracts whose ownership structure responds to probate timing. The data cannot separate these sources.

The remaining rows of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} show that the sign holds across transformations of the ownership measure. Each row uses the same two probate-timing instruments and changes the endogenous ownership measure. Measures that increase with concentration, such as the largest owner's share, have positive coefficients. Measures that increase with fractionation, such as the inverse largest-owner share and the log number of owners, have negative coefficients. The log-owner row gives an intuitive scale. Its coefficient is $-0.0334$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.024, so doubling the owner count lowers lease income per trust acre by 2.3 percent. These alternatives are robustness checks. The preferred measure remains $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$, because under Hypothesis 2 monitoring incentives depend on the stake of the owner with the strongest private incentive to monitor. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:count_iv_robustness} reports row-specific first-stage diagnostics for the owner-count alternatives. Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} also reports two broader concentration measures. The cumulative share of the top five owners has a coefficient of 0.246, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.021, and the ownership Herfindahl index has a coefficient of 0.332, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.023.

The Crow-included columns of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} show how much the institutional sample definition matters. Including Crow gives a coefficient of $-0.097$, with a standard error of 0.055 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.107. The Anderson--Rubin interval runs from $-0.212$ to 0.040, and the conditional likelihood ratio set from $-0.224$ to 0.018. The estimate is 30 percent smaller than without Crow and imprecise, which is consistent with the competency-leasing regime placing some Crow agricultural leases outside the BIA leasing process that the model describes.

The preferred sample pools S tracts, whose trust estate holds the surface only, and B tracts, whose trust estate also holds the minerals (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:trust_estate}, Panel B). Estimated separately, the S tracts give $-0.234$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.020, and the B tracts give $-0.072$, with a standard error of 0.106 and a $p$-value of 0.558. Their difference is 0.138, with a standard error of 0.119 and a $p$-value of 0.252, so the data do not distinguish the two estimates. Without the indicator for B tracts, the pooled estimate is $-0.132$, with a $p$-value of 0.032.

Conditional independence is the central identifying assumption. Reservation fixed effects remove broad differences in allotment era, climate, terrain, agency practice, and rental markets across reservations. They also remove the variation driven by the fact that a reservation opened to allotment in the 1880s began dividing tracts decades before one opened in 1910. Lease-signing year fixed effects remove common rent conditions, commodity-price movements, and production conditions in the year the lease was signed. This matters especially for the latest-probate instrument because a death shortly before a lease begins can move the latest probate date and the lease date together. After these fixed effects and controls, the design compares tracts on the same reservation and in the same lease-signing year whose family deaths and estate processing occurred at different times. The no-direct-effect condition is distinct. It would fail if probate timing changed BIA attention or lease administration through a channel other than ownership fractionation.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:instrument_timing} checks whether the instruments are predetermined relative to the lease by comparing the preferred exact-date rule, which uses probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date, with adjacent constructions. A year-level construction that uses probate-order years no later than the lease year gives $-0.156$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.004, and a stricter prior-year construction gives $-0.131$, with a $p$-value of 0.023. The latest-through-2010 construction gives $-0.121$, with a $p$-value of 0.002, but it can use probate orders approved after the lease term begins and is therefore not the preferred design. The earliest-only construction drops the latest-probate instrument. That just-identified estimate is $-0.220$, with a standard error of 0.109, a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.059, and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.456$ to $-0.012$.\footnote{Grazing permits provide a placebo for the no-direct-effect condition. Within a permit, the permittee, the administratively set rate, the signing date, and the agency staff are the same for every tract, so ownership should not affect the rent the BIA allocates to a tract. Across 17,842 tracts in 1,295 permits on 16 reservations, the within-permit estimate with both instruments is $-0.038$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.42, or 27 percent of the agricultural-lease estimate. In the reduced form, the coefficient on the earliest order is $-0.0008$ per year, with a standard error of 0.0007, and the coefficient on the latest order is $-0.0011$, with a standard error of 0.0004. The earliest order therefore passes the placebo, while the latest order predicts slightly lower allocated rent, 1.1 percent per decade.} These checks support the lease-effective-date construction without replacing it.

Holding land quality fixed shows which instrument the estimate rests on (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_quality_sensitivity}, Panel A). With the soil measures as controls, the earliest-only estimate is $-0.219$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.008, and the latest-only estimate is $-0.049$, with a $p$-value of 0.344. Conditional on soil quality, the effect therefore rests on how long a tract has been fractionated, which the earliest order measures. The earliest order is also the instrument most exposed to the allottee's longevity. Among current owners, however, the rate of probate orders after lease signing shows no relation to any soil measure (Panel C), and the longevity bias that the estimated relation implies is at most 13 percent of the estimate.\footnote{We simulate tracts from allotment to the April 2010 snapshot, with owners' mortality falling with the soil index at the rate estimated in Panel C and income rising with it at the rate the data give. At the estimated rate, the bias is 0.003. At the bound of its 95 percent interval, a hazard 3.1 percent lower per within-reservation standard deviation of the index, the bias is $-0.019$, or 13 percent of the preferred estimate. These figures cover the measured soil component of land fertility and assume that the mortality gradient of current owners also held for the allottees. Better rangeland has fewer pending estates per owner in 2009 (Panel C) but not fewer completed probate orders, which fits faster probate of valuable estates better than lower mortality.}

Longer lags reduce the role of probate orders processed close to the lease (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel A). Requiring both instruments to precede the lease by at least three, five, or ten years yields coefficients of $-0.150$, $-0.145$, and $-0.147$. These estimates imply 40-to-20 effects of 9.4, 9.1, and 9.2 percent. Each first stage remains strong, each wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value is below 0.05, and each Anderson--Rubin and conditional likelihood ratio confidence set excludes zero. The estimates are stable across lags, so the effect does not depend on probate orders processed close to lease start.

Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} then separates inheritance events from later administrative paperwork. A probate order creates new heirs and divides an ownership interest. A probate modification revises an existing order without necessarily adding owners. The main instruments use probate orders only. When we rebuild the instruments using both orders and modifications, the coefficient is $-0.141$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.016 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.206$ to $-0.051$. The result therefore reflects inheritance events, not later paperwork revisions.

A second threat is that probate timing may proxy for allotment cohort within a reservation. Cohorts allotted at different times could differ in soil quality, allotment layout, or regulatory history. TAAMS does not record trust-patent dates before 1912, so we replace reservation fixed effects with fixed effects for the reservation and the decade of a tract's earliest recorded conveyance (Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust}). For 94.1 percent of leases, that decade is the decade of the earliest probate order, so these fixed effects absorb most of the earliest order's variation and the estimate rests mainly on the latest order. The coefficient is $-0.076$, with a standard error of 0.054, against $-0.139$ in the baseline. The estimate is 55 percent of the headline and imprecise, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.187 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.151$ to 0.057, on 6,301 leases and 43 reservations.

The estimate is similar or slightly larger with stronger local fixed effects (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel B). On a common 6,329-lease sample, replacing additive reservation and signing-year fixed effects with reservation-by-signing-year fixed effects moves the coefficient from $-0.136$ to $-0.149$. On a common 6,158-lease sample, adding county-by-signing-year fixed effects alongside reservation fixed effects moves the coefficient from $-0.142$ to $-0.143$. Both stronger specifications retain strong first stages and weak-instrument-robust confidence sets that exclude zero. These variants absorb time-varying reservation or county conditions, including some variation in local administrative capacity and rental markets. Because parsed county only approximates tract location, we keep the additive specification as preferred.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_characteristics_sensitivity} separates basic land endowments from soil measures and reports instrument balance on both. On a common sample of 6,039 leases on 42 reservations, the specification without added land controls gives a coefficient of $-0.146$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.014. Adding slope, elevation, surface sand, and surface clay gives $-0.133$, with a $p$-value below 0.001 and a 40-to-20 effect of 8.3 percent. With these controls the two instruments disagree, with an overidentification $p$-value of 0.021, so the Anderson--Rubin set is empty, while the conditional likelihood ratio set runs from $-0.220$ to $-0.064$. Adding four soil productivity and condition measures gives $-0.094$, with a $p$-value of 0.046, an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.157$ to $-0.029$, and a 40-to-20 effect of 6.0 percent. The first stage hardly changes, with effective $F$-statistics of 89.2 without land controls and 87.4 with the soil measures, so the attenuation comes from the reduced form. The largest partial correlation between an instrument and a land measure is 0.042 for terrain and 0.035 for soil, and one of eight balance tests in each battery has a $p$-value below 0.05.

We keep the estimate without land controls as the headline because it uses all 6,337 leases and does not require the soil measures to be unaffected by a tract's history, which they can reflect through mapped erosion and drainage phases. Two sensitivity bounds show how large a violation of the identifying conditions would have to be (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:land_quality_sensitivity}, Panel B). Under the bounds of \citet{ConleyHansenRossi2012}, the union of confidence intervals excludes zero for direct effects of probate timing on income up to 15 percent of each instrument's reduced-form coefficient, and the local-to-zero interval up to 32 percent. Selection on unobserved land quality would have to be 5.8 times as strong as selection on the soil measures to remove the reduced form \citep{Oster2019}. At equal selection, the bias-adjusted estimate is $-0.078$.

A second ownership snapshot provides a short-run measurement check (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ownership_snapshot_validation}). The September 2009 file links to 99.98 percent of tracts in the preferred sample. Across linked tracts, correlations between the September 2009 and April 2010 ownership measures range from 0.9937 to 0.9995, and the top owner is unchanged for 93.5 percent of tracts. On the identical sample of 5,310 leases effective by September 28, 2009, the IV coefficient is $-0.1082$ when ownership is measured in September 2009 and $-0.1077$ when it is measured in April 2010. The coefficients differ by 0.46 percent, and the 40-to-20 translations differ by 0.030 percentage points. Both estimates are imprecise on this smaller sample, with wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values of 0.111 and 0.112, but the ownership measure is stable over the horizon the two snapshots cover. The sample splits below examine leases signed earlier in the window.

Leases signed in 2006--2008 are furthest from the April 2010 ownership snapshot, and reservations differ in how completely their probate records are recorded. Two sample splits therefore check whether measurement drives the estimate (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_additional_diagnostics}, Panel C). The coefficient is $-0.158$ for leases signed from 2006 through 2008 and $-0.110$ for leases signed in 2009 or 2010, and both estimates are imprecise. The estimate is $-0.081$, with a standard error of 0.075 and a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.350, in reservations below the median of record completeness. In reservations above the median it is $-0.276$, with a standard error of 0.050 and a $p$-value of 0.0003. There the two instruments disagree, with an overidentification $p$-value of 0.005, so the Anderson--Rubin set is empty, while the conditional likelihood ratio set runs from $-0.476$ to $-0.094$. The estimate is therefore larger where records are more complete. That pattern fits attenuation from incomplete records, but it could also reflect differences in the effect across reservations. The difference between the two halves is $-0.190$, with a standard error of 0.095 and a conventional cluster-robust $p$-value of 0.052.

The main estimate also survives changes to the probate window, sample restrictions, and outcome trimming. Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_robust} shows that removing the probate-date eligibility cutoff leaves the coefficient at $-0.138$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.013. Adding the direct-payment leases back gives $-0.164$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.011. Keeping the full positive-income sample without the 1/99 outcome trim gives $-0.123$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.031. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_outlier_appendix} reports additional trimming variants, and the 2/98 and 5/95 trims give $-0.139$ and $-0.164$. Figure~\ref{fig:iv_spec_curve} summarizes these checks and the corresponding Crow-included comparisons. Dropping each reservation one at a time gives estimates between $-0.183$ and $-0.123$, with no change of sign, and 43 of the 44 are statistically significant at the 5 percent level under conventional cluster-robust inference.

Taken together, the diagnostics support the preferred probate-timing design. The evidence combines a strong first stage, reduced-form signs consistent with the model, a stable negative two-stage least-squares estimate, robustness to timing and sample definitions, and no single reservation driving the result. As in any IV design, conditional independence and the no-direct-effect condition cannot be tested directly. The main remaining threats are residual family histories, record quality, unobserved tract attributes, and direct administrative channels linking probate processing to lease administration. The estimate is also sensitive to measured soil quality, which lowers it by 36 percent on the common sample of 6,039 leases.

The estimates apply to a specific population. The broader lease records are close to a census of multi-owner agricultural leasing of allotted trust land in the contiguous United States. The preferred IV estimate applies to agency-pay, multi-owner leases with post-1938 qualifying probate histories on the 44 non-Crow reservations in the preferred sample. These reservations lie in 12 states, and Montana, the Dakotas, and Oklahoma account for 85.7 percent of the leases. Of the 3,853 leases on reservations that \citet{Dippel2014} classifies, 3,526 are on forced-coexistence reservations and 327, on 3 reservations, are not. Another 2,484 leases on 20 reservations have no classification. The 327 leases outside forced coexistence are too few to test whether the effect depends on that history. The estimate does not extend to Crow leases covered by the competency-leasing regime, wholly tribal land, land that was never allotted, unleased land, grazing permits, or oil and gas leases. The mechanism is general, but the magnitude is specific to this setting.

\subsection{Owner count and free-riding} \label{Mechanism}

The model attributes the income loss to free-riding: as ownership divides among more heirs, each owner captures a smaller share of the return to monitoring. Under equal shares, equation~\eqref{(5)} states this prediction in terms of the number of owners, and the owner-count rows of Table~\ref{tab:table_iv_main} have the predicted sign. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_owner_count} asks where in the ownership distribution the effect arises by splitting the preferred IV sample at 30 owners, into 1,276 leases with 2 to 30 owners and 929 leases with more.

The effect is largest among tracts with 2 to 30 owners. In that range, the coefficient on the log owner count is $-0.174$, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.009 and an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.283$ to $-0.090$, so doubling the owner count lowers lease income per trust acre by about 11 percent. Above 30 owners the estimate is $-0.024$, with an Anderson--Rubin interval from $-0.323$ to 0.253. That interval contains the estimate for 2 to 30 owners, so the difference between the two ranges is not statistically significant. The square root of the owner count gives the same pattern, and so does a least-squares spline in the multi-owner sample, whose slope per owner is about 9 times as steep below 30 owners as above, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.097 for the difference in slopes. The concentration is also consistent with the imprecise linear owner count in Table~\ref{tab:frac_compact}, which gives the most weight to the most fractionated tracts, where an additional owner matters least.

The same split using $1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}$ points in the same direction, but within 2 to 30 owners the two probate instruments disagree: the over-identification test rejects, with a $p$-value of 0.028, and the Anderson--Rubin set is empty. We therefore state the mechanism in owner-count terms. All of these estimates use the same instruments and the same reduced form, so the alternative measures express one causal relationship on different scales.

A descriptive check addresses a different explanation, divided attention. Many owners also hold interests in other agricultural tracts on the same reservation, and an owner spread across many tracts might attend less to each. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:divided_attention} holds within-tract fractionation fixed and relates lease income to how many other tracts the tract's owners hold. Owners' holdings elsewhere are not associated with significantly lower income, whether measured for all owners, for the largest owner, who is the would-be monitor in the model, or for the other owners. The evidence ties the income loss to division within the tract rather than to owners' commitments elsewhere, as the free-riding mechanism predicts.

\subsection{Monitoring costs and owner involvement} \label{OtherPredictions}

Under the maintained IV assumptions, the probate-timing estimates provide the paper's causal evidence on ownership fractionation. This subsection studies three secondary margins descriptively: whether the tribe is the largest owner, whether lease payments are made directly to owners, and the fee-simple share of the tract. The question is whether these associations line up with lower monitoring costs or greater owner involvement.

Whether the tribe is the largest owner is the most direct proxy for lower monitoring costs. Tribes have formal government-to-government channels with the BIA and may be better positioned than individual co-owners to monitor the agency. In the organized-owner extension (Section~\ref{Theory} and Appendix~A), such an owner can supply all monitoring without holding the largest share if its monitoring cost is low enough. Table~\ref{tab:table4} relates log lease income per trust acre to an indicator for whether the tribe is the largest owner. Columns 1 and 2 include reservation and lease-signing year fixed effects and then add fee-simple share. Column 3 adds the lease controls. Column 4 repeats the richer specification in the multi-owner sample. The coefficient ranges from 0.062 to 0.088 across columns, with wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values between 0.010 and 0.027. The richer full-sample estimate in column 3 implies about 9 percent higher lease income per trust acre when the tribe is the largest owner.

Figure~\ref{fig3} shows the same pattern in binned residualized data for tracts with positive tribal ownership. Lease income rises with tribal share, and a binscatter linearity test does not reject a linear relationship at conventional levels ($p=0.07$). The figure shows that the Table~\ref{tab:table4} association extends beyond the binary largest-owner threshold. The appendix checks show that the largest-owner margin carries more information than tribal presence alone.\footnote{Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table5} separates tribal share, largest-owner status, and their interaction. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table_play1} replaces the largest-owner indicator with an indicator for any tribal ownership, which is small and not statistically distinguishable from zero in the richest specification. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:table_play4} reports tribal-share functional forms on tracts with positive tribal ownership. The linear tribal-share association is positive, while the inverse-square-root form is not.}

Direct payment is the second proxy for owner involvement. Table~\ref{tab:table8} reports regressions using either the directly paid share or the direct-payment indicator, both defined in Section~\ref{Data}. Column 1 uses the directly paid share in the full sample. A full-range increase in the directly paid share is associated with 0.220 log points higher lease income per trust acre, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.031. Equivalently, a 10-percentage-point larger directly paid share is associated with about 0.022 log points higher income. Column 2 replaces the continuous measure with a direct-payment indicator. The coefficient falls to 0.148, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.103. The continuous measure is therefore more informative than the binary indicator, which groups leases with small directly paid shares together with leases paid almost entirely to owners.

Columns 3 and 4 show that the direct-payment association remains positive in more relevant subsamples. Excluding Crow raises the continuous estimate to 0.256, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.002. Restricting to the multi-owner sample gives 0.251, with a wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-value of 0.068. These magnitudes are consistent with the owner-involvement interpretation.

Direct payment is nevertheless hard to interpret as a treatment. First, it is an indirect measure of involvement: the BIA approves the lease under either payment arrangement, and the data do not record who negotiated the lease. Second, Figure~\ref{fig4} shows that direct-payment leases are less fractionated than agency-pay leases. The median direct-payment lease has seven owners, compared with 17 owners for agency-pay leases, so direct payment combines owner involvement with the ownership structure studied above. Third, many directly paid rents appear as round hundred-dollar amounts, which suggests coarser recording than for agency-paid rents. For these reasons, we treat the direct-payment coefficient as descriptive evidence on owner involvement.

Fee-simple share is the least direct of the three descriptive margins, and its link to trust income runs through the lessee. The BIA does not lease fee interests or collect rent for fee owners, and it does not condition approval of a lease of the trust interests on a lease from the fee owners.\footnote{25 C.F.R. \S 162.102(c) (2011), the rule in force when the sample leases were signed. The current rule, 25 C.F.R. \S 162.004(a), adds that the lessee is responsible for accounting to the owners of any fee interests.} Because every interest is an undivided share of the whole tract, a lessee who farms the tract must also deal with the fee owners, on terms set outside the BIA records. Fee owners hold unrestricted title and negotiate for themselves, so a larger fee-simple share brings owners with a direct stake in the terms into the same transaction. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fee_simple_appendix} relates log lease income per trust acre to the fee-simple share of the tract. Across the richer specifications, a 10-percentage-point larger fee-simple share is associated with roughly 0.027 to 0.030 log points higher lease income per trust acre, with wild-cluster bootstrap $p$-values close to 0.06. The positive sign is consistent with direct owner involvement raising the rent on the whole tract, but the data do not record the fee owners' terms, so the association cannot separate that channel from other differences between tracts with and without fee interests.

Taken together, the tribal largest-owner and direct-payment results provide the clearest descriptive evidence on monitoring costs and owner involvement. The fee-simple association points in the same direction but is less directly tied to the model.

\subsection{Magnitude and calibration} \label{Counterfactual}

The main causal magnitude is the 40-to-20 largest-owner-share effect: under the preferred probate-timing IV design, moving from a 40 percent largest-owner share to a 20 percent share lowers lease income per trust acre by about 14 percent. Two supplementary exercises put the estimates on a dollar scale. The first applies the preferred IV design to lease income measured in dollars. The second uses a no-controls levels regression to map the model into monitoring and output gaps. The second exercise is descriptive and does not provide separate causal identification.

Table~\ref{tab:calibration_crowex} reports the model-based calibration. The Crow-excluded column uses 8,387 leases and matches the preferred institutional population; it is broader than the preferred IV sample. The Crow-included column is a comparison. The competitive ceiling $R_l$ is lease income per trust acre when bureaucratic effort no longer constrains the lease.\footnote{We mean the competitive lease income attainable on the reservation. Federal restrictions and tribal regulation can create a wedge between on- and off-reservation rents.} We estimate this ceiling using the mean income on single-owner leases negotiated independently of the BIA and the intercept from the model-implied levels regression
\begin{equation} \label{(24)}
    income_{ijt}=\beta_0+\beta_1 \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{\max}}}\right)+\varepsilon_{ijt},
\end{equation}
\noindent The single-owner, independently negotiated leases average \$25.73 per trust acre. The Crow-excluded intercept is \$25.44, and the Crow-included intercept is \$24.76. In the Crow-excluded calibration, the slope is $-1.22$ and the implied responsiveness parameter is $\gamma=0.058$. The constrained efficient monitoring level is \$1.16 per trust acre, or about \$141 per lease-year. Evaluated at the mean largest-owner share, actual monitoring is \$0.72 per trust acre, or about 62 percent of the constrained efficient level. The associated output gap is \$0.69 per trust acre, 3.0 percent of mean lease income, or roughly \$85 per lease-year. We treat this as a lower benchmark.

The preferred dollar-outcome IV implies a model gap of about \$260 per lease-year when evaluated at the mean largest-owner share and about \$390 when averaged across leases. Applying the preferred log-IV estimate to observed lease income and trust acreage gives a larger range, roughly \$430 to \$670 per lease-year, depending on the evaluation convention. These figures compare observed ownership with the constrained efficient benchmark; they are not the dollar value of the 40-to-20 contrast.

Figure~\ref{fig6} plots the model-implied output gap from this calibration against the largest-owner share. The gap falls as the largest-owner share rises because a larger stake raises the private return to monitoring. The solid curve uses the Crow-excluded calibration, and the dashed curve reports the Crow-included comparison.

\section{Conclusion}

Beneficiary monitoring can shape bureaucratic output when one agency decision produces a shared return. Each beneficiary bears the cost of contacting the agency, supplying information, requesting better terms, or monitoring compliance, but receives only a share of the gain. The model shows that dispersed beneficiary stakes weaken private monitoring incentives and lower the output a bureaucracy secures.

We study this problem in agricultural leases for allotted trust land administered by the BIA. Ownership fractionation is the product of more than a century of inheritance, so least-squares estimates may reflect land productivity, original allottee characteristics, or family history. The preferred IV design instruments fractionation with the earliest and latest qualifying probate orders approved no later than the lease effective date. Under the maintained IV assumptions, reducing the largest owner's share from 40 percent to 20 percent lowers lease income per trust acre by about 14 percent in the Crow-excluded sample. This is the paper's main causal magnitude and is local to the ownership margins shifted by probate timing. The effect is largest among tracts with 2 to 30 owners, and owners' holdings on other tracts do not account for it.

Targeted diagnostics support this design. The estimate remains negative when probate orders must precede the lease by three, five, or ten years, when the specification absorbs reservation-by-year or county-by-year conditions, and when it controls for basic terrain and surface soil texture. Ownership measures are also stable between September 2009 and April 2010. Adding soil productivity measures, which may partly reflect the effect of fractionation itself, reduces the 40-to-20 effect to 8.8 percent (Section~\ref{Identification}). The IV identifies the total effect of fractionation on lease income, which includes any effect through accumulated investment as well as through monitoring.

The mechanism points to two classes of institutional responses. Consolidating fractional interests into tribal or fewer individual hands can raise the private return to monitoring. A body that represents the co-owners in their dealings with the BIA can instead aggregate monitoring without changing every ownership share. The BIA already acts for the owners as trustee, but it is the agent whose effort the owners monitor, so it cannot also be the body that monitors on their behalf. Estimating the effects of these reforms is a natural next step. The mechanism points toward ownership concentration or representation within trust, not fee-simple conversion. Fee conversion changes control and ownership rights, and allotment-era conversion out of trust produced large-scale loss of American Indian land.

The estimates come from agricultural leasing of allotted trust land, and Section~\ref{Identification} sets out where they apply. The mechanism is more general. Whenever one agency decision produces a shared return, dividing the claims also divides the incentive to watch the agency. On allotted trust land, generations of inheritance have done exactly that.

\subsection*{The Organized-Owner Extension}
\setcounter{equation}{0}
\renewcommand{\theequation}{A\arabic{equation}}
\renewcommand{\theHequation}{A\arabic{equation}}

An organized owner is an owner whose organizational form or formal access to the agency lowers the unit cost of monitoring. In the application, a tribal owner is the leading example. This appendix extends the theoretical model by allowing owners to differ in monitoring costs as well as in output shares. Section~\ref{Theory} shows that, with identical monitoring costs, the largest ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring in equilibrium. With unequal costs, the relevant object is the cost-adjusted ownership share.

We consider two owners who may contribute monitoring: an organized owner $g$ and an individual owner $I$, the individual with the largest ownership share. Other individual owners have weakly smaller shares and the same unit monitoring cost as owner $I$, so none has a larger cost-adjusted ownership share than $I$. It is therefore sufficient to compare owner $g$ with owner $I$. Aggregate monitoring is $M=m_g+m_I$.

Normalize the organized owner's unit cost of monitoring to 1, and let owner $I$'s unit cost be $c_I\geq 1$. The organized owner's payoff is $\pi_g=s_gR-m_g$, where $s_g$ is its output share and $m_g$ is its monitoring contribution. Owner $I$'s payoff is $\pi_I=s_I R-c_I m_I$, where $s_I$ is that owner's output share and $m_I$ is that owner's monitoring contribution.

For any owner $k$ with output share $s_k$ and unit monitoring cost $c_k$, the monitoring level it would choose if the other owner contributed zero is $\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_k/c_k}-\gamma$. The object $s_k/c_k$ is the cost-adjusted ownership share. The best response for owner $I$ is
\begin{equation} \label{(11)}
    m_I = \max\left\{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma-m_g,0\right\},
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the best response for the organized owner is
\begin{equation} \label{(12)}
    m_g = \max\left\{\sqrt{R_l \gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma-m_I,0\right\}.
\end{equation}

The equilibrium depends on the comparison between $s_g$ and $s_I/c_I$. If $s_g>s_I/c_I$, the organized owner has the larger cost-adjusted ownership share. In equilibrium, the organized owner contributes all monitoring and owner $I$ contributes zero. Aggregate monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(13)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
and output is
\begin{equation} \label{(14)}
   R=R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_g}}\right).
\end{equation}
The constrained efficient benchmark remains $M^* = \sqrt{R_l \gamma} - \gamma$. Because the organized owner has the lowest unit monitoring cost, a planner assigns all monitoring to that owner. The output gap is therefore
\begin{equation} \label{(15)}
    R^*-R = -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}+\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_g}}\right).
\end{equation}

If $s_I/c_I>s_g$, owner $I$ has the larger cost-adjusted ownership share. In equilibrium, owner $I$ contributes all monitoring and the organized owner contributes zero. Aggregate monitoring is
\begin{equation} \label{(16)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma,
\end{equation}
\noindent
output is
\begin{equation} \label{(17)}
   R=R_l -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{c_I}{s_I}},
\end{equation}
\noindent
and the output gap is
\begin{equation} \label{(18)}
   R^*-R = -\sqrt{R_l\gamma}+\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{c_I}{s_I}}.
\end{equation}

If $s_g=s_I/c_I$, the two owners have the same cost-adjusted ownership share. The equilibrium allocation of monitoring between them is not unique. Any nonnegative split between $m_g$ and $m_I$ that delivers total monitoring
\begin{equation} \label{(19)}
   M=\sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{s_g}-\gamma = \sqrt{R_l\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{s_I}{c_I}} -\gamma
\end{equation}
\noindent
is an equilibrium and yields the same output.

The extension preserves the public-goods logic of the main model. With equal costs, the largest ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring in equilibrium. With unequal costs, the cost-adjusted ownership share determines which owner contributes monitoring. Monitoring and output rise with that owner's ownership share and fall with that owner's unit monitoring cost. A lower monitoring cost can make an organized owner contribute all monitoring even when its ownership share is below that of the largest individual owner.

\subsection*{Crow Competency-Leasing Regime and the IV Sample}

Section~\ref{Econometrics} explains why the preferred IV sample excludes the Crow Reservation. Under 25 C.F.R. \S~162.600, Crow Indians classified as competent may lease their trust lands and their minor children's trust lands for farming or grazing without Secretarial approval. The same regulation allows competent Crow Indians to elect Secretarial assistance and requires Secretarial approval for leases signed by Crow Indians not classified as competent and for certain inherited or devised trust lands. Part 153 governs the procedures for determining competency of Crow Indians \citep{ECFR25CFR162600,ECFR25CFR153}.

\subsection*{BIA Agricultural Leasing Procedures}

This appendix documents the procedures that governed the agricultural leases in our sample and illustrates them with BIA documents. The leases were signed between 2006 and 2010 under 25 C.F.R. Part 162 as issued in 2001, and the 2012 revision of Part 162 did not change the agricultural-lease rules \citep{DOI2001LeasingRule, BIA2012}. The BIA's operative guidance in these years was its procedural handbook on leasing and permitting, issued in March 2006 \citep{BIA2006Ch1, BIA2006Ch2}.

\paragraph{Leasing cycle.} Table~\ref{tab:leasing_steps} summarizes the steps. Realty staff identify the leases that will expire in the next leasing cycle 10 to 14 months in advance and obtain a market survey, rent study, or appraisal for allotted land. A full appraisal is the exception, used mainly for negotiated leases and rent adjustments \citep[pp.~6--7]{BIA2006Ch2}. Each owner then receives a notice that the owners may negotiate a lease within 90 days (Exhibit~A). The notice adds that, failing an acceptable lease, the superintendent may advertise the tracts for sealed bids and grant a lease on behalf of all co-owners. Owners who negotiate receive owner lists, appraisal or market-rent information, and consent forms, and in most cases the prospective lessee initiates the negotiation \citep[pp.~8--9]{BIA2006Ch2}. Tracts not leased by negotiation are advertised by mail or public posting, and bids are opened within 30 days. The highest bidder normally wins, the superintendent may reject any or all bids, and staff record every bid in an abstract of the sale \citep[pp.~15--17]{BIA2006Ch2}. Exhibit~B reproduces the BIA's sample sale notice.

\paragraph{Rent.} The BIA determines fair annual rental by competitive bidding, appraisal, or another appropriate valuation method, following professional appraisal standards (25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.101, 162.211). The regulations prescribe no rate schedule for agricultural leases. The agency's valuation is the minimum for bids and the reference point for negotiated rents, and the rent on a lease is the winning bid or the negotiated amount \citep[pp.~15--17]{BIA2006Ch2}. A single bid below the valuation may be raised orally at the sale (Exhibit~B), and after advertising the BIA may lease to the highest responsible bidder below its appraisal (25 U.S.C. \S 3715(a)(2) and 25 C.F.R. \S 162.222(d)). Within reservations and signing years, rents per trust acre in our data are dispersed rather than concentrated on a few schedule values (Table~\ref{tab:leasing_facts}, Panel~B).

\paragraph{Terms and re-letting.} Table~\ref{tab:leasing_facts} also reports lease terms and rent changes in the lease records. Most leases run five years and end the day before an anniversary of their start (Panel~A). Rent is usually unchanged within a lease, and it changes at re-letting for 95.3 percent of the leases that we can link to a predecessor (Panel~C).

\paragraph{Payment and enforcement.} Rent is due on dates set in the lease, normally once a year in advance, whether or not the lessee receives a bill (25 C.F.R. \S\S 162.224 and 162.247, and \citealp[Attachment~12A]{BIA2006Ch2}). The BIA may inspect leased land at any reasonable time (25 C.F.R. \S 162.250). Its handbook asks for an inspection of each lease at least once a year \citep[Attachment~5A]{BIA2006Ch2}, and the TAAMS extract contains no inspection dates.

\paragraph{Owner information and appeals.} Owners receive a notice when the BIA intends to lease their land (Exhibit~A) and a notice of approval with appeal rights, and they receive a copy of the lease on request (25 C.F.R. \S 162.214(b) and \citealp[pp.~20--21]{BIA2006Ch2}). An owner whose interests could be adversely affected by a leasing decision may appeal within 30 days to the regional director and then to the Interior Board of Indian Appeals (25 C.F.R. \S\S 2.2, 2.4, and 2.9 (2008)). Lease approvals take effect immediately, notwithstanding an appeal (25 C.F.R. \S 162.216).

\paragraph{Exhibits.} We did not locate the 2006--2010 lease-sale notices of the agencies in our sample. Exhibits~A and~B are the BIA's national sample forms from its 2006 handbook \citep[Attachments~9A and~14A]{BIA2006Ch2}, issued under the 2001 rule that governed every lease in our sample. The handbook blanks the agency's name and the contact details in Exhibit~B, and we omit the contact paragraph and signature line that close it.
